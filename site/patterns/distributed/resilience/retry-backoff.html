Network · Resilience

Retry with Backoff

Waits progressively longer between attempts — usually with a dash of randomness — so a fleeting failure gets a chance to clear instead of being hammered by an instant retry.

Distributed Resilience

What it is

Retry with Backoff wraps a call that can fail transiently and, on failure, waits before trying it again — each wait longer than the last, typically growing exponentially (base × 2^attempt) up to some ceiling. Control returns to the caller only on success, once attempts are exhausted, or the moment an error is recognized as non-transient and not worth retrying at all.

The force it resolves is a mismatch between failure and response speed. A dropped packet, a momentary overload, a lock held a beat too long — these clear on their own, and an immediate retry often succeeds. But immediate, synchronized retries from many clients at once do the opposite of helping: they pile onto a dependency at the exact moment it's already struggling, turning a brief blip into a self-inflicted retry storm. Backoff spreads that load out over time; adding jitter — randomizing the delay rather than following a fixed curve — desynchronizes clients further so they don't all retry in lockstep.

Backoff alone isn't sufficient. Each attempt still needs its own Timeout / Deadline, or a hung attempt stalls the whole retry loop instead of failing into it. And because retrying re-executes the call, safety depends on Idempotency — a retried write that isn't idempotent can duplicate its effect the moment a response is lost after the operation actually succeeded.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Some calls fail for a passing reason — a moment of network trouble, or a service saying it is too busy right now — and would have worked a second later. So try again, but wait before each attempt, and wait longer each time. It is what you do when a phone line is engaged: you do not redial every second, partly because everyone redialing at once is a large part of why nobody is getting through.

Advanced

Re-execute a failed call after a growing wait — a base delay doubling per attempt, capped at a ceiling and randomized with jitter so clients that failed together do not retry together. Only transient errors qualify; a bad request or an auth failure should fail immediately. In a KYC screening system the delay is a column, not a sleep: a failed vendor call writes an attempt count and a pushed-out run_after onto the task row, the worker moves on, and whichever replica claims that row next is the one that retries.

Expert

Retry only where the failure is plausibly transient and the operation is idempotent or dedup-keyed, since a retried write whose success response was lost duplicates its effect. Backoff buys automatic recovery from blips and pays for it with a slower, far less predictable failure path — and it is most dangerous exactly where it looks most useful: unbounded or un-jittered retries amplify load on a dependency already struggling, and retries nested at client, gateway and service multiply together. Budget them at one tier, cap attempts and delay, trip a breaker on sustained failure, and let a server's own Retry-After outrank your curve.

How it works

Variations

Fixed delay
Retry after the same interval every time. Simplest to reason about, but it doesn't ease off under sustained failure and can still synchronize a flood of retries.
Exponential backoff
Each retry's wait multiplies the last, usually doubling, capped at a maximum delay so the curve doesn't grow unbounded on a long outage.
Jittered backoff
Randomize the delay around the backoff curve — full jitter, equal jitter, or decorrelated jitter — so clients that failed at the same instant don't all retry at the same instant too.
Retry budgets
Cap total retry volume as a share of overall traffic, independent of any single caller's attempt count, so retries collectively can never outgrow the load they're meant to smooth over.
Server-directed timing
Honour a delay the server hands back — a Retry-After header or a protocol-level pushback signal — and let it win over the locally computed wait. The overloaded side knows when it wants traffic back; doubling your own curve against its advice retries on the wrong schedule.
Declarative retry
The curve is configuration on the step, not a loop inside the handler. A Step Functions retrier declares which errors it matches plus the first interval, the attempt cap, the multiplier, a ceiling on the interval and whether to jitter — so what counts as retryable and how long the tail runs are reviewable in one place instead of scattered across call sites. It also moves the wait off your own process, which is what you want when the retryable unit is a durable step rather than an in-flight request.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Resolves transient blips automatically, without ever surfacing an error to the caller.
  • Spaces out load over time, giving a struggling dependency room to recover instead of adding to the pile-up.
  • Jitter desynchronizes clients so a shared outage doesn't turn into a synchronized retry storm.
  • Drops into any call site with no changes required on the callee's side.

Cons

  • Only helps with transient failures — retrying a permanent error just delays it and wastes time.
  • Naive or unbounded retries under a real outage amplify load exactly when the dependency needs it least.
  • Requires idempotent operations, or a retried write can duplicate its side effects.
  • Adds latency variance — the failure path gets slower, and less predictably so, with every attempt.

When to use it

Reach for it when

  • The failure is plausibly transient — network blips, brief overload, lock contention, rate limiting.
  • The operation is idempotent, or safe to retry via a dedup key.
  • You control the client and can afford some extra latency on the failure path.

Avoid when

  • The failure is permanent — a bad request, an auth failure, a missing resource — retrying just delays the error.
  • The write isn't idempotent and there's no dedup mechanism to make a repeat safe.
  • The dependency is visibly down for a while — pair with a Circuit Breaker so retries stop adding to the pile-up.

Code sketch

TypeScript — backoff with full jitter, written to a queue column 
const MAX_ATTEMPTS = 5, BASE_MS = 200, MAX_MS = 15 * 60_000;

// The delay is a column, not a sleep. The worker hands the row back and moves
// on; whichever replica claims it after run_after is the one that retries.
function nextRunAfter(attempts: number): Date {
  const backoff = Math.min(MAX_MS, BASE_MS * 2 ** attempts);
  return new Date(Date.now() + Math.random() * backoff); // full jitter
}

async function runTask(db: Db, task: Task): Promise<void> {
  try {
    await execute(task);          // call idVendor, or deliver the client webhook
    await db.query(`UPDATE task SET status = 'done' WHERE id = $1`, [task.id]);
  } catch (err) {
    const attempts = task.attempts + 1;
    if (!isTransient(err)) {
      await db.query(
        `UPDATE task SET status = 'dead', attempts = $2 WHERE id = $1`,
        [task.id, attempts]);
      return;
    }
    // Transient: back to pending with a later run_after. Once attempts are
    // exhausted it simply stops being claimed, and the sweeper escalates it.
    await db.query(
      `UPDATE task SET status = 'pending', locked_at = NULL,
              attempts = $2, run_after = $3
        WHERE id = $1`,
      [task.id, attempts, nextRunAfter(attempts)]);
  }
}

// Claiming honours the schedule — a backed-off task is not yet visible at all.
const claim = `SELECT * FROM task
                WHERE status = 'pending' AND run_after <= now()
                  AND attempts < ${MAX_ATTEMPTS}
                ORDER BY run_after
                FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1`;

function isTransient(err: unknown): boolean {
  return err instanceof NetworkError || err instanceof RateLimitError
      || err instanceof VendorUnavailable;   // breaker open — ask again later
}

In the wild

AWS SDKThe retry curve is configuration, not code: retry_mode picks standard, the default, which retries transient and throttling errors with exponential backoff and jitter bounded by max_attempts (3 by default). Its retry quota is a token bucket charged per retry and refunded on success, so sustained failure drains it and the client fails fast instead of adding load to a service already struggling. Adaptive mode adds a client-side rate limiter that can delay even the initial request once the service signals throttling — useful against one hot resource, harmful on a client that fans out across many.
p-retryThe Node wrapper for the same curve as options rather than a loop: retries (10 by default), factor (2), minTimeout (1 s), maxTimeout (unbounded) and randomize for jitter. A shouldRetry predicate and an AbortError stop the loop immediately on an error that will never succeed — the non-transient case backoff must never paper over.
gRPCService config declares a retryPolicy with maxAttempts, initialBackoff, maxBackoff, backoffMultiplier, and the set of retryable status codes, applied by the client library — plus channel-level retry throttling (a token bucket) that disables retries when failures dominate, guarding against storms.
KubernetesThe kubelet restarts a crashing container on an exponential back-off that doubles each failure up to a five-minute cap, surfaced as the CrashLoopBackOff state until a start succeeds.

In production

Tuning knobs

  • Max attempts / retry budget — The cap on attempts per call, and ideally a fleet-wide retry budget (retries as a share of total traffic) so retries collectively cannot outgrow the load they smooth.
  • Base delay and multiplier — The initial wait and its growth factor (base × 2^attempt) — the shape of the backoff curve.
  • Max delay ceiling — A cap on the per-attempt wait so the exponential curve does not grow unbounded during a long outage.
  • Jitter strategy — Full, equal, or decorrelated jitter randomizes the delay so clients that failed together do not retry together.
  • Retryable classification — Which errors count as transient and worth retrying (timeouts, 429, 503) versus permanent ones (400, 401, 404) that should fail immediately.

Signals to watch

  • Retry rate / amplification — Retries as a fraction of total requests — how much extra load the retry layer is adding, especially during an incident.
  • Success-after-retry rate — Whether retried calls actually recover; a low rate means the failures are not transient and retrying is just waste.
  • Failure-path latency — The added tail latency (p99) contributed by waiting through the backoff curve before giving up.

Failure modes under load

  • Retry storm — Unbounded or un-jittered retries pile onto a struggling dependency at the worst moment, turning a blip into a self-inflicted outage.
  • Cross-layer amplification — Retries nested at multiple tiers multiply — client × gateway × service — so three attempts each becomes many times the original load.
  • Non-idempotent duplication — Retrying a write whose success response was lost duplicates its effect unless the operation is idempotent or dedup-keyed.
  • Retrying permanent errors — Retrying a 4xx or an auth failure never succeeds; it only delays the error and wastes the attempt budget.

Readiness checklist

  • Cap attempts and set a max-delay ceiling so backoff cannot grow unbounded.
  • Add jitter to desynchronize clients that failed at the same instant.
  • Classify errors and retry only transient ones — never a 4xx client error.
  • Ensure the operation is idempotent or dedup-keyed before retrying a write.
  • Pair with a circuit breaker so retries stop when a dependency is sustainedly down.
  • Budget retries at a single tier to avoid cross-layer amplification.
