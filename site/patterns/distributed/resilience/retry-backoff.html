Network · Resilience

Retry with Backoff

Waits progressively longer between attempts — usually with a dash of randomness — so a fleeting failure gets a chance to clear instead of being hammered by an instant retry.

Distributed Resilience

What it is

A call fails, and by the time you read the error the reason for it has already passed — a dropped packet, a lock held a beat too long, a service that was busy for a second. Running the same call again works. Running it again immediately, from every client whose call failed at that same instant, does not: they all arrive together and finish off a dependency that was only stumbling. Retry with Backoff tries again after a wait, and waits longer before each further attempt.

The loop wraps one call and classifies what comes back. A transient failure — a timeout, a 429, a 503 — waits base × 2^attempt, capped at a ceiling and randomized with jitter, then re-executes. A permanent one — a malformed request, a rejected credential — returns to the caller straight away and spends no attempt. Control comes back on success, on a permanent error, or once the attempt budget is gone; and where the wait is long, the delay belongs on a scheduled row rather than in a held thread.

What the growing delay buys is a thinner second wave. Retries are load, and they arrive exactly when the dependency has least to spare — un-jittered, they arrive in lockstep, so a blip the system would have absorbed becomes an outage the retries themselves keep alive.

Backoff alone isn't enough. Each attempt needs its own Timeout / Deadline, or a hung attempt stalls the loop instead of failing into it. And the operation has to be idempotent, because a retry after a lost success response repeats an effect that already happened.

Explained at three levels

Basic

A call fails for a reason that has already gone away — the network hiccupped, or the service on the other end was busy for a moment. Ask again and it works. The trap is asking again straight away: everyone whose call failed at that instant asks again at that instant too, and a service that was briefly busy is now properly buried. So wait before trying again, and wait longer each time — the way you would not redial an engaged number every second, since constant redialling is a good part of why nobody is getting through. After a few tries, stop and say the call failed, rather than trying forever.

Advanced

Classify the failure, then re-execute after a growing wait: a base delay doubling per attempt, capped at a ceiling and randomized with jitter so clients that failed together do not retry together. Only transient errors qualify — a timeout, a 429, a 503; a malformed request or a rejected credential returns immediately and spends no attempt. The variants differ in where the wait lives and who chooses it. A fixed interval is the simplest and never eases off under sustained failure; a server's own Retry-After outranks any curve you compute locally; and for work that outlives a request the delay belongs on a queue row rather than a sleeping thread — write an attempt count and a pushed-out run_after, release the worker, and whichever replica claims that row next is the one that retries.

Expert

Retry where the failure is plausibly transient and the call is idempotent or dedup-keyed. Where it is neither, fail and hand the outcome to a compensating transaction or a dead-letter channel — a retry loop over a non-repeatable write buys availability with duplicated effects. The bill comes in three parts, each with a counter-move. The failure path gets slower and much less predictable, so cap the attempts and the ceiling and bound every attempt with a timeout, checked against the caller's remaining deadline. Retries are load arriving at the worst moment, so jitter the curve, cap retries as a share of traffic, and trip a circuit breaker once failure stops being transient. And retries nested at client, gateway and mesh multiply — three tiers of three attempts is 27 calls for one request — so pick the tier that owns retry and turn the others off. Then run the loop: tune the curve and the retryable set, watch retry rate against success-after-retry, find the tier that amplifies first under load, and gate the release on a dedup key for every write the loop can repeat.

How it works

Variations

Fixed delay
Retry after the same interval every time. Simplest to reason about, and the only variant that never eases off as the failure persists.
Exponential backoff
Each retry's wait multiplies the last, usually doubling, capped at a maximum delay so the curve doesn't grow unbounded on a long outage.
Jittered backoff
Randomize the delay around the backoff curve — full jitter, equal jitter, or decorrelated jitter — so clients that failed at the same instant don't all retry at the same instant too.
Retry budgets
Cap total retry volume as a share of overall traffic, independent of any single caller's attempt count, so retries collectively can never outgrow the load they're meant to smooth over.
Server-directed timing
Honour a delay the server hands back — a Retry-After header or a protocol-level pushback signal — and let it win over the locally computed wait. The overloaded side knows when it wants traffic back; doubling your own curve against its advice retries on the wrong schedule.
Declarative retry
The curve is configuration on the step, not a loop inside the handler. A Step Functions retrier declares which errors it matches plus the first interval, the attempt cap, the multiplier, a ceiling on the interval and whether to jitter — so what counts as retryable and how long the tail runs are reviewable in one place instead of scattered across call sites. It also moves the wait off your own process, which is what you want when the retryable unit is a durable step rather than an in-flight request.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Resolves transient blips automatically, without ever surfacing an error to the caller.
  • Spaces out load over time, giving a struggling dependency room to recover instead of adding to the pile-up.
  • Jitter desynchronizes clients so a shared outage doesn't turn into a synchronized retry storm.
  • Drops into any call site with no changes required on the callee's side.

Cons

  • Only helps with transient failures — retrying a permanent error just delays it and wastes time.
  • Unbounded or un-jittered retries amplify load exactly when the dependency has least to spare — cap the attempts, jitter the curve, and trip a circuit breaker once the failure stops being transient.
  • Requires idempotent operations, or a retried write duplicates its effect the moment a success response goes missing — carry a dedup key on anything the loop can repeat.
  • Adds latency variance — the failure path gets slower, and less predictably so, with every attempt, so bound the whole loop with the caller's deadline and not just each attempt.
  • Retries nested at client, gateway and mesh multiply — three tiers of three attempts is 27 calls for one request. Pick the tier that owns retry, turn it off in the others, and record which one it is.

When to use it

Reach for it when

  • The failure is plausibly transient — network blips, brief overload, lock contention, rate limiting.
  • The operation is idempotent, or safe to retry via a dedup key.
  • You control the client and can afford some extra latency on the failure path.

Avoid when

  • The failure is permanent — a bad request, an auth failure, a missing resource — retrying just delays the error.
  • The write isn't idempotent and there's no dedup mechanism to make a repeat safe.
  • The dependency is visibly down for a while — pair with a Circuit Breaker so retries stop adding to the pile-up.

Code sketch

TypeScript — the smallest retry loop that works, waiting in this process 
const MAX_ATTEMPTS = 5, BASE_MS = 200, MAX_MS = 30_000;

const sleep = (ms: number) => new Promise(done => setTimeout(done, ms));

async function withRetry<T>(call: () => Promise<T>): Promise<T> {
  for (let attempt = 0; ; attempt++) {
    try {
      return await call();                       // the call carries its own timeout
    } catch (err) {
      const lastTry = attempt + 1 >= MAX_ATTEMPTS;
      if (lastTry || !isTransient(err)) throw err;   // permanent: fail now, not later
      const backoff = Math.min(MAX_MS, BASE_MS * 2 ** attempt);
      await sleep(Math.random() * backoff);      // full jitter: anywhere in 0…backoff
    }
  }
}

await withRetry(() => verifyDocument(personaId, deadline));
TypeScript — backoff with full jitter, written to a queue column 
const MAX_ATTEMPTS = 5, BASE_MS = 200, MAX_MS = 15 * 60_000;

// The delay is a column, not a sleep. The worker hands the row back and moves
// on; whichever replica claims it after run_after is the one that retries.
function nextRunAfter(attempts: number): Date {
  const backoff = Math.min(MAX_MS, BASE_MS * 2 ** attempts);
  return new Date(Date.now() + Math.random() * backoff); // full jitter
}

async function runTask(db: Db, task: Task): Promise<void> {
  try {
    await execute(task);          // call idVendor, or deliver the client webhook
    await db.query(`UPDATE task SET status = 'done' WHERE id = $1`, [task.id]);
  } catch (err) {
    const attempts = task.attempts + 1;
    if (!isTransient(err)) {
      await db.query(
        `UPDATE task SET status = 'dead', attempts = $2 WHERE id = $1`,
        [task.id, attempts]);
      return;
    }
    // Transient: back to pending with a later run_after. Once attempts are
    // exhausted it simply stops being claimed, and the sweeper escalates it.
    await db.query(
      `UPDATE task SET status = 'pending', locked_at = NULL,
              attempts = $2, run_after = $3
        WHERE id = $1`,
      [task.id, attempts, nextRunAfter(attempts)]);
  }
}

// Claiming honours the schedule — a backed-off task is not yet visible at all.
const claim = `SELECT * FROM task
                WHERE status = 'pending' AND run_after <= now()
                  AND attempts < ${MAX_ATTEMPTS}
                ORDER BY run_after
                FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED LIMIT 1`;

function isTransient(err: unknown): boolean {
  return err instanceof NetworkError || err instanceof RateLimitError
      || err instanceof VendorUnavailable;   // breaker open — ask again later
}

In the wild

AWS SDKThe retry curve is configuration, not code: retry_mode picks standard, the default, which retries transient and throttling errors with exponential backoff and jitter bounded by max_attempts (3 by default). Its retry quota is a token bucket charged per retry and refunded on success, so sustained failure drains it and the client fails fast instead of adding load to a service already struggling. Adaptive mode adds a client-side rate limiter that can delay even the initial request once the service signals throttling — useful against one hot resource, harmful on a client that fans out across many.
p-retryThe Node wrapper for the same curve as options rather than a loop: retries (10 by default), factor (2), minTimeout (1 s), maxTimeout (unbounded) and randomize for jitter. A shouldRetry predicate and an AbortError stop the loop immediately on an error that will never succeed — the non-transient case backoff must never paper over.
gRPCService config declares a retryPolicy with maxAttempts, initialBackoff, maxBackoff, backoffMultiplier, and the set of retryable status codes, applied by the client library — plus channel-level retry throttling (a token bucket) that disables retries when failures dominate, guarding against storms.
KubernetesThe kubelet restarts a crashing container on an exponential back-off that doubles each failure up to a five-minute cap, surfaced as the CrashLoopBackOff state until a start succeeds.

In production

Tuning knobs

  • Max attempts — How many tries a call gets before the caller is told it failed — the hard end of the curve.
  • Base delay and multiplier — The first wait and its growth factor (base × 2^attempt) — the shape of the curve.
  • Max delay ceiling — A cap on the per-attempt wait, so the exponential does not run away during a long outage.
  • Jitter strategy — Full, equal or decorrelated jitter — how much randomness is mixed into each delay.
  • Retryable classification — Which errors count as transient (timeouts, 429, 503) and which fail straight through (400, 401, 404).
  • Retry budget — A fleet-wide cap on retries as a share of total traffic, independent of any one caller's attempt count.

Signals to watch

  • Retry rate — Retries as a fraction of total requests, per dependency — the extra load the retry layer is adding right now.
  • Success-after-retry rate — The share of retried calls that eventually succeed. A low share means the failures were never transient.
  • Attempt distribution — How deep into the budget calls are going. A shift from attempt 1 to attempt 3 is the dependency degrading before it fails.
  • Failure-path latency — The p99 of calls that exhaust the curve — what a user waits for an error.
  • Server pushback rate — How often the callee returns 429 or Retry-After, and whether your schedule respects the delay it asks for.

Failure modes under load

  • Retry storm — Un-jittered retries return in lockstep and pile onto a dependency at the worst moment, so the blip is sustained by the retries themselves.
  • Cross-layer amplification — Retries nested at client, gateway and mesh multiply, so a few attempts each becomes tens of calls for one request.
  • Non-idempotent duplication — A success response lost in transit looks like a failure, so the retry repeats an effect that already happened — a second charge, a second email.
  • Budget burnt on permanent errors — Every attempt spent on a 4xx that will never succeed is an attempt the transient failures do not get.
  • Retrying past the caller's deadline — The client gave up mid-curve, so the remaining attempts do work nobody is waiting for — pure load with no possible payoff.

Readiness checklist

  • Every operation the loop can repeat carries a dedup key or is provably idempotent, verified by replaying a call whose success response was dropped
  • The retryable error set was checked against the errors this dependency actually returns, not the HTTP defaults
  • Exactly one tier retries this call and the others are configured off, written down where the next team will find it
  • A load test with the dependency made slow, not switched off, shows retry volume staying inside the budget
  • Retry rate and success-after-retry are exported per dependency, with an alert on retries climbing while successes do not
  • Exhausted retries land somewhere a person sees — a dead-letter channel or an escalation — rather than a log line
  • The caller's remaining deadline is checked before each attempt, so no attempt starts that cannot finish in time
