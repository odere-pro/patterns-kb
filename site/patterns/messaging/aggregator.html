Lens · Messaging

Aggregator

Collects a stream of related messages that arrive independently over time and combines them into one complete message once every piece — or a deadline — has arrived.

Messaging Lens

What it is

An aggregator is a stateful filter that receives a stream of individual messages, recognizes which ones belong together, and combines them into a single aggregate message before passing it on. It leans on three pieces: a correlation identifier shared by every message in the set, an aggregation strategy that folds the payloads into one, and a completeness condition that decides when the set is done and the combined message should be released.

The force it resolves is that related data often doesn't arrive as one unit. A batch job emits one message per line item, a fan-out request gets one reply per recipient, a saga collects one event per step — and none of those senders knows or cares about the others. Without an aggregator, every consumer would have to re-implement the same bookkeeping: hold state, watch for the rest of the set, and decide when it's safe to act.

The aggregator centralizes that bookkeeping in one place. Producers stay simple — they just emit a message with a correlation id — and consumers downstream see one coherent result instead of a pile of fragments they'd otherwise have to reconcile themselves.

Its hardest problem isn't combining data, it's knowing when to stop waiting. A set that never completes because one message is lost will hold state forever unless the completeness condition accounts for that possibility.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Related messages arrive one at a time and out of order — the lines of one order, the quotes from the three suppliers you asked. A step that acts on the first message it sees is deciding on a third of the answer, and if every step waits on its own, each one keeps the same tally in its own slightly different way. Put one step in the middle that holds the early messages until the rest turn up, then passes on a single finished result. It works like a parcel locker: boxes for the same address keep arriving, and the door opens once the delivery is complete — or once the deadline passes and you take what came.

Advanced

That tally has three moving parts. A correlation key decides which arrivals belong together, an aggregation strategy folds each new message into the partial result held for that key, and a completeness condition decides when to release it and discard the state. Four conditions cover the field — an expected count, an end-of-sequence marker, a wall-clock timeout, and a predicate over what has accumulated — and most deployments pair a count with a timeout so one lost fragment cannot hold the group open. The partial results live in a store, in memory for speed or in a database so they survive a restart, and the number of groups in flight is the pattern's real resource: one entry each, released only on completion. It is the gather half of scatter-gather, and the usual partner of a splitter that stamped every fragment with its parent id, sequence and total.

Expert

Reach for it when fragments arrive independently and every consumer would otherwise reimplement the same tally; skip it when each message stands on its own, because a collector buys nothing and charges latency and state for it. The completeness condition is the decision that carries the design — it fixes the latency floor, since nothing emits before the last fragment or the deadline, and it fixes what an incomplete set is allowed to mean. Three costs follow, each with a counter-move. State sits on the hot path and a crash takes every open group with it — back the store with a durable repository, and acknowledge a fragment on the source channel only once it is folded in. A key that never completes holds its entry forever — always set a completion timeout, and bound the number of concurrent groups so an unbounded key space cannot exhaust memory. Duplicates corrupt the fold and a late arrival opens a spurious one-item group — make the fold idempotent per fragment id, and decide explicitly whether a post-release arrival is dropped or sent to a dead-letter channel. Then run the loop: tune the timeout against observed fragment latency, watch open groups and the age of the oldest, expect the never-completing key to break first, and gate on emitting whether each aggregate closed on its condition or on its deadline.

How it works

Variations

Count-based completeness
Release once a known number of related messages has arrived — simplest when the set size is fixed or announced up front.
Timeout-based completeness
Release whatever has accumulated once a deadline passes, so one missing or late fragment can't block the aggregate forever.
Best-effort aggregation
Combine on timeout even when incomplete, marking the missing pieces rather than discarding a partial result outright.
Scatter-Gather
Broadcast a request to several recipients and aggregate their replies into one — the aggregator is the gather half of that round trip.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Collapses a stream of related messages into one coherent result the rest of the system can consume simply.
  • Correlation id decouples producers — none of them needs to know about the others or about the aggregation itself.
  • Count- and timeout-based completeness make it resilient to slow, missing, or reordered fragments.
  • Centralizes reconstruction logic in one place instead of duplicating it in every downstream consumer.

Cons

  • Needs a durable correlation store — a crash mid-aggregation must not silently drop the partial state.
  • Choosing completeness is a real design problem: too strict waits forever, too loose emits partial data as if it were whole.
  • Adds latency — nothing downstream sees anything until the last piece, or the timeout, arrives.
  • Duplicate or out-of-order inputs complicate the aggregation strategy and must be handled explicitly.

When to use it

Reach for it when

  • Multiple independently-arriving messages share a correlation id and only make sense combined — line items, partial responses, sharded events.
  • Downstream consumers want a single coherent message per business event, not N fragments to reconcile themselves.
  • You're gathering replies from a fan-out and need one result from the set — the classic scatter-gather round trip.

Avoid when

  • Messages don't share a natural, stable key to correlate on — there is nothing to group them by.
  • Each message must be processed independently and immediately; waiting for the rest of the set defeats that.
  • The transformation needed is stateless, one message in and one message out — a plain translation step is enough, with no collector required.

Code sketch

TypeScript — a minimal count-based aggregator 
interface CorrelatedMessage<T> {
  correlationId: string;
  payload: T;
}

class Aggregator<T, R> {
  private groups = new Map<string, T[]>();

  constructor(
    private readonly expectedSize: number,
    private readonly combine: (parts: T[]) => R,
    private readonly onComplete: (result: R) => void,
  ) {}

  add(msg: CorrelatedMessage<T>): void {
    const parts = this.groups.get(msg.correlationId) ?? [];
    parts.push(msg.payload);
    this.groups.set(msg.correlationId, parts);

    if (parts.length >= this.expectedSize) {
      this.groups.delete(msg.correlationId); // release and forget
      this.onComplete(this.combine(parts));
    }
  }
}

In the wild

Apache Camel aggregate()The aggregate() EIP takes a correlation expression, an AggregationStrategy, and a completion condition — completionSize, completionTimeout, completionPredicate, or completionInterval. An optional AggregationRepository (JDBC, Infinispan, LevelDB) persists open groups so a restart does not lose them.
Spring Integration AggregatorGroups messages by correlation key in a MessageStore and releases the combined message when its ReleaseStrategy is satisfied; a group-timeout forces release of a partial group so one missing message cannot hold it open indefinitely.
Kafka StreamsWindowed aggregations group records by key and fold them with an initializer and aggregator into one result. The window closes after a configured grace period, after which a late record is dropped rather than reopening the already-emitted window.

In production

Tuning knobs

  • Completion condition — What triggers release of a group — a message count, a predicate over the accumulated set, or a periodic interval (Camel completionSize / completionPredicate / completionInterval, Spring release strategy).
  • Completion timeout — A deadline after which an incomplete group is released anyway, so one lost or slow fragment cannot hold it open forever (Camel completionTimeout, Spring group-timeout).
  • Correlation expression — The key expression that decides which messages belong to the same group — the join key on which all bookkeeping hangs.
  • Aggregation store — Where open groups are held: an in-memory map, or a durable repository (Camel AggregationRepository backed by JDBC/Infinispan, Spring MessageStore) that survives a process restart.
  • Open-group bound — A ceiling on how many correlation groups may be in flight at once, so an unbounded stream of keys that never complete cannot exhaust memory — enforced by the store's capacity or an eviction/timeout policy rather than one dedicated switch.

Signals to watch

  • Open correlation groups — Count of incomplete sets currently held in the store — a direct proxy for aggregation-state memory.
  • Age of oldest open group — How long the longest-waiting set has been incomplete; a rising floor means fragments are being lost or delayed.
  • Timeout vs. completion releases — Ratio of aggregates released on the deadline (incomplete) versus on the completion condition (whole) — a shift toward timeouts signals missing fragments.

Failure modes under load

  • Group that never completes — A correlation key missing one fragment, with no completion timeout, holds its partial state indefinitely and leaks memory.
  • Partial state lost on restart — An in-memory store drops every open group when the process restarts; fragments already consumed from the source channel are gone for good.
  • Late fragment after release — A message arriving after its group already released either opens a spurious new single-item group or is silently discarded.
  • Timeout too tight — Under load, fragments that are merely slow miss the deadline and the aggregator emits incomplete sets as if they were whole.

Readiness checklist

  • Set a completion timeout so a single lost fragment cannot hold a group open forever.
  • Use a durable aggregation store if losing partial groups on restart is unacceptable.
  • Decide and implement behaviour for fragments that arrive after their group has completed.
  • Bound or alert on the number of concurrent open correlation groups to cap memory.
  • Emit whether each aggregate was released by its completion condition or by timeout.
