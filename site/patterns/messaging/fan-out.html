Fan-Out

Fan-out delivers a single message as a separate, independent copy to every interested consumer, so each one processes it in parallel at its own pace.

Messaging

What it is

Fan-out is a delivery topology: one published message is replicated into an independent copy for every consumer, and each consumer processes its own copy in parallel — one way, with no reply expected and no results merged back. The producer emits once; the messaging fabric — a topic, an exchange, a broker — does the multiplying.

The force it resolves is a producer that must inform many consumers of the same fact — an order was placed — where those consumers have nothing to do with one another. Notifying them by a hard-coded list of direct calls couples the producer to every consumer's address, availability, and speed, and lets the slowest or most broken of them stall the rest. Reach for fan-out specifically when it matters that the copies are independent — buffered, retried, and consumed separately — not merely that the sender is decoupled, which is what publish-subscribe on its own already buys you.

Explained at three levels

Basic

One thing happens — an order is placed — and four unrelated systems need to hear about it: billing, search, email, analytics. Call them one after another and the producer waits for the slowest, and the one that is down takes the other three notifications with it. Publish the fact once instead, and let the messaging fabric hand each system its own copy to work through in its own time. A slow reader holds nobody up, a broken one fails alone, and nobody is expected to send an answer back.

Advanced

Each copy usually lands in its own durable queue behind a topic or exchange, and that queue is the isolation boundary: its own backlog, its own in-flight window, its own retry count, its own dead-letter queue. Delivery is at-least-once per copy and the branches share nothing, so every consumer needs its own idempotent handler. Three dials distinguish deployments. A branch can take the whole stream or attach a subscription filter that trims it to the attributes it cares about. The broker can push each copy to an endpoint, or let the consumer poll and pull at the rate it can sustain. And ordering, where you need it, is preserved per key at the cost of some of the parallelism that made the topology worth using.

Expert

Reach for it when the consumers are genuinely unrelated, because total time then tracks the slowest single branch instead of the sum of all of them. Skip it when a message must reach exactly one of several workers — that is a queue with competing consumers — and skip it when you need the answers back, which is Scatter-Gather. Three costs come with the topology, each with a counter-move. Amplification is the first: one logical event becomes N deliveries, N retry budgets and N handlers, and a publish burst lands on every consumer at the same moment — attach subscription filters so a branch never carries traffic it does not need, and size each branch's consumer pool for the burst rather than the average. The second is that no global completion signal exists, so did-everyone-get-it cannot be read off the publish — answer it with per-branch backlog and oldest-message age, and alert on each branch's dead-letter depth separately. The third is silent loss: a wrong filter drops messages and nothing errors anywhere — test filter rules the way you test code, and reconcile per-branch delivery counts against publish counts. Then run the loop: tune filters, in-flight window and batch size; watch per-queue backlog, oldest-message age and dead-letter depth; expect one branch to back up alone while the rest stay healthy; and gate on every consumer being idempotent before the first duplicate arrives.

How it works

Variations

Pure broadcast vs. filtered fan-out
Every consumer receives every message, or each subscription carries a filter — by attribute or by content — so a branch only sees messages it cares about, trimming volume and cost per consumer.
Direct fan-out vs. queue-per-consumer hybrid
The topic pushes straight to each consumer, or a durable queue sits in front of each one; the queue absorbs bursts and lets a slow branch fall behind without holding up the fast ones.
Push vs. poll delivery
The broker pushes each copy to a consumer's endpoint, or each consumer polls its own queue and pulls at the rate it can actually handle.
Ordered vs. unordered fan-out
Delivery is best-effort parallel with no order across messages, or ordering is preserved per key at the cost of some of the parallelism.

Trade-offs

Pros

  • Consumers process in parallel, each at its own pace, so total time tracks the slowest single branch, not the sum of them.
  • A durable queue per consumer isolates one slow or failed branch from all the others.
  • New consumers attach to the topic without touching or redeploying the producer.
  • Filtering keeps each branch's volume relevant instead of forcing every consumer to see everything.

Cons

  • One logical message becomes N physical deliveries — load and cost that grow with the number of consumers.
  • Each branch needs its own idempotency, retry, and dead-lettering; delivery is at-least-once per copy.
  • There is no built-in "did everyone get it" — completion and failure are per-branch, not global.
  • A misconfigured filter silently drops messages a consumer needed, with nothing in the call to trace.
  • Per-branch isolation stops at the broker: a consumer that never catches up piles its unread copies into storage every branch shares, until a backlog limit or an expiry policy cuts it off.

When to use it

Reach for it when

  • Several independent consumers must each act on the same event, in parallel and without coordinating.
  • Consumers run at different speeds and one must be free to fall behind without stalling the others.
  • You want to add or remove consumers without redeploying or even informing the producer.

Avoid when

  • Each message must go to exactly one of several workers, not all of them — that is a message queue, not a broadcast.
  • You need the combined result of asking many parties — that is Scatter-Gather, which adds a correlated gather.
  • A strict synchronous response is required before the producer can continue.

Left unbounded, fan-out is also how a single publish becomes a thundering herd — every consumer hit at once — so size the branches for the amplification before it finds you.

Code sketch

TypeScript — fan one message into isolated per-consumer branches 
type Message = { type: string; body: unknown };

interface Consumer {
  name: string;
  wants(m: Message): boolean;        // per-subscription filter
  handle(m: Message): Promise<void>;
}

// The producer publishes once; each consumer gets its own independent copy.
async function fanOut(consumers: Consumer[], message: Message): Promise<void> {
  const deliveries = consumers
    .filter((c) => c.wants(message))            // trim the branch to what it cares about
    .map((c) => c.handle(structuredClone(message))); // an independent copy per branch

  // one slow or failing branch never blocks delivery to the others
  const results = await Promise.allSettled(deliveries);

  results.forEach((r, i) => {
    if (r.status === "rejected") {
      // retry or dead-letter this branch alone — the rest already went through
      console.error(`branch ${consumers[i].name} failed:`, r.reason);
    }
  });
}

In production

Tuning knobs

  • Per-subscription filter — Which messages a branch receives — narrows a consumer to the subset it cares about instead of the whole stream.
  • In-flight / visibility timeout — How long a delivered copy is hidden from redelivery while a consumer works it; too short redelivers prematurely, too long slows recovery from a crash.
  • Redelivery limit before dead-lettering — How many times a copy is retried on one branch before it is moved to that branch dead-letter queue (a maxReceiveCount-style dial).
  • Delivery retry / backoff policy — How the fabric spaces retries to a failing branch so a struggling consumer is not hammered.
  • Per-consumer batch size — How many copies a consumer pulls and processes per fetch, trading latency against throughput on that branch.

Signals to watch

  • Per-queue backlog — Pending copies on a single branch; the core per-consumer saturation signal (e.g. an approximate-messages-visible count).
  • Age of the oldest unprocessed copy — How far the slowest branch has fallen behind the head of its queue.
  • Dead-letter-queue depth — Copies a branch gave up on after exhausting retries — poison messages or a broken consumer.
  • Delivery-failure / throttle rate — How often the fabric fails or is throttled delivering to a branch endpoint.

Failure modes under load

  • One branch backs up in isolation — A slow or failed branch sees its queue grow while the others stay healthy — the isolation win, visible as a single climbing backlog.
  • Poison message redelivery loop — A copy that always fails redelivers on its branch until the redelivery limit moves it to the dead-letter queue.
  • Silent filter drop — A misconfigured filter excludes messages a consumer needed; nothing errors, the branch simply never sees them.
  • Publish-burst amplification — A burst on the topic multiplies into a simultaneous copy on every branch, hitting all consumers at once.

Readiness checklist

  • Every consumer is idempotent — delivery is at-least-once per copy
  • Each branch has its own dead-letter queue and someone watches its depth
  • Filter rules are tested — a wrong filter drops messages silently
  • Per-queue backlog and oldest-message age are monitored per branch
  • Fan-out breadth is sized for the N-fold amplification of a publish burst
