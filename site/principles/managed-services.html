Principle

Prefer Managed Services

Give the operational burden of a commodity capability — a database, a queue, a cache, an identity system — to someone whose full-time job is running it. Engineering attention is the scarcest thing you have, and spending it on infrastructure nobody buys from you is a decision, whether or not you made it deliberately.

Principle

What it says

Where a capability is a commodity, rent it rather than run it. A database, a message broker, a cache, an object store and an identity system are each a standing engineering commitment — patching, version upgrades, backups somebody has actually restored, failover you hope works, capacity planning, and the on-call rotation underneath all of it. Almost none of that is what your users pay you for, so hand it to a provider who does it all day and spend the attention you get back on the part only you can build.

This is an argument about where scarce engineering attention goes, not a licence to stop understanding your infrastructure. It does not say never operate anything; it says make every decision to operate something an explicit one. Build it yourself when it is a differentiator, when no offering meets a requirement you can state precisely, or when the numbers genuinely stop working at your scale — and when none of those three holds, the honest answer is that you wanted to.

Explained at three levels

Basic

You need somewhere to keep data, so you install a database on a machine and it works. Then it wants security patches, and backups, and somebody awake when the disk fills at midnight — none of which is what your customers are paying for. Meanwhile a company sells exactly that capability, run by people who do nothing else all day. Pay them, and spend the time you get back on the part of the product only you can build.

Advanced

The trade is explicit: money and some control, in exchange for engineering attention and a better failure floor. What you hand over is patching, version upgrades, backup and restore, replication across failure domains, automatic failover and capacity headroom — the tasks that make a small team's rotation miserable and that a provider spreads across thousands of customers. The judgement is per capability rather than per system. Rent where the capability is a commodity with an interface everyone already agrees on: relational storage, object storage, caching, message brokers, identity. Build where it is the thing you sell, where no offering meets a requirement you can write as a test, or where your own scale has moved the arithmetic.

Expert

Price four costs at adoption, while you still have the option not to. Switching cost is designed in rather than accidental, so reach the service through an interface your domain owns and keep the exit estimated — without reimplementing its features to stay neutral, which discards what you rented. The cost curve can cross: pricing that makes a small workload trivial may exceed hardware plus salary at a large steady one, so re-run the numbers each time scale moves by an order of magnitude, and count the rotation and the incidents on the build side or the comparison is fiction. Its limits become yours, so test the requirement that worries you against the real quota before you commit, not after. And its outages become yours with no lever to pull, which makes a second path a deliberate decision per capability rather than something you discover during the incident.

Why it helps

The work you avoid is the work that never appears in a plan. Nobody estimates the afternoon lost to a minor-version upgrade that went sideways, the sprint spent proving the backups actually restore, or the year of nights on a rotation for a broker that four people understand. That is what running a commodity yourself costs, and it is paid in exactly the hours you would otherwise have spent on the product.

The second thing you buy is a floor under your worst day. A managed store that replicates across failure domains, takes point-in-time backups and fails over without a human gives you a durability posture a small team reaches only after several incidents have taught it what to build. Renting costs a line item you can see and argue about; building costs those incidents, and you pay for them in customer trust rather than in currency.

Applying it

Decide it capability by capability, never once for the whole system:

  • Write down what your users actually pay you for, and defend that list. Everything on it deserves your best engineers; everything off it is a candidate for somebody else to run, and the burden of proof sits with keeping it in-house.
  • Start with the storage tiers, which carry the most operational weight per unit of differentiation. A managed relational database, a hosted Object Storage service and a Distributed Cache you never patch remove the three most common sources of unplanned weekend work.
  • Rent the plumbing next. A hosted broker behind a Message Queue, or a managed API Gateway in front of your services, replaces two systems that are cheap to stand up and expensive to keep healthy under real traffic.
  • State the requirement before you shop, as something an offering passes or fails. “Writes acknowledged inside a named jurisdiction” or “a ninety-day immutable audit trail” is a test; “it might not be flexible enough” is how a team talks itself into a build it will still be maintaining in five years.
  • Keep your code portable at the seam, not throughout. Reach the service through an interface your own domain owns, so replacing it means writing one adapter — but do not reimplement its features to stay neutral, because a lowest-common-denominator wrapper discards the reason you rented it.
  • Shape your own tier so the rented one can move underneath it. A Stateless Service that holds no session and no local durable state lets you resize instances, migrate a store or ride a provider failover without a migration plan for your own processes.
  • Re-run the arithmetic whenever scale moves by an order of magnitude, and put salaries in it. Compare the invoice against hardware plus the engineers, the rotation and the incidents — a build costed on the infrastructure line alone is nearly always mispriced, and a rental that was obvious at a tenth of today’s traffic may no longer be.

None of this is laziness dressed up as strategy. It is Keep It Simple and You Aren’t Gonna Need It applied one level down from the code: run the least infrastructure that satisfies a requirement you can name out loud.

Taken too far

Managed services are cheap to enter and expensive to leave, and that asymmetry is part of the product rather than an accident of it. The bill can curve the wrong way too: pricing that makes a small workload trivial may cross over the cost of running the same thing yourself once the workload is large and steady, and the crossing is invisible unless somebody is watching for it. Estimate the exit and sketch the growth curve at the moment you adopt, while you still have the option not to.

A provider’s limits become your limits. A cap on payload size, a maximum retention, a query timeout you cannot raise: each is harmless until a requirement lands on the wrong side of it, and then it is an architecture problem rather than a configuration one. Its outages become your outages as well, with no lever for you to pull — you cannot roll back somebody else’s deployment, and the honest thing to tell your own users during that hour is that you are waiting. Decide in advance which capabilities deserve a second path and which ones you accept being down with, because that choice gets made either way.

The subtlest cost is the understanding you never acquire. Debugging eventually needs a model of how the thing behaves — how the storage engine plans that query, what the broker does when one consumer stalls — and a team that has never run one has nothing to reason with when the managed version misbehaves in a way the console does not explain. Some decisions are also not yours to make: data-residency rules, an air-gapped deployment or a certification a customer demands can rule an offering out whatever its engineering merits. Keep enough depth to reason about what you depend on, treat the provider’s documentation as required reading rather than optional, and notice the day “we use the managed one” stops being a decision and becomes a Golden Hammer.
