Principle

Build for the Needs of the Business

How much downtime the product may take, how fast it must recover, how much data it may lose and what it may cost are business decisions with a business owner — not engineering preferences settled by taste. A number nobody wrote down still gets set, by whoever was writing the code that night, and the assumption surfaces during the incident.

Principle

What it says

Treat the non-functional requirements as requirements. How much downtime a year the product can tolerate, how long recovery may take, how much data may be lost in a failure and what the whole thing may cost each belong to someone in the business; engineering's job is to say what each choice buys and what it costs, then build to the number that comes back. Skip the conversation and the numbers still get set — an engineer assumes one while choosing a database, and everyone finds out which one during the outage.

This is not the argument about building less. Whether a capability should be built at all is YAGNI's question, and how elaborately to build it is KISS's; both are about scope and neither is settled here. What this principle asks comes earlier and is narrower: which properties the system must hold to, written as figures a business owner would recognise and put their name against, before anyone draws the first box.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Someone will ask for a system that is always up. Nobody means that literally, and the gap between what they meant and what you assumed shows up on the worst night of the year — when the service comes back after two hours and it turns out one hour was the limit, or when yesterday's orders are gone and nobody had said they mattered. Ask for the figures at the start instead: how much downtime is allowed, how long recovery may take, and how much data may be lost.

Advanced

Those figures are what pick the architecture, so get them before you draw it. An availability target converts straight into a downtime budget — 99.9% is about 43 minutes a month — and each further nine cuts that by ten while ruling out first manual recovery, then maintenance windows, then any release that cannot roll itself back. The recovery pair works the same way: how long you may take to return decides whether standby capacity runs warm and whether failover waits for a human, while how much data you may lose decides whether replication is synchronous, which is a round trip charged on every write rather than only during the incident. Cost belongs on the same list, stated as a ceiling with a budget owner beside it.

Expert

Two numbers have to stay apart: what you promise a customer, which carries a penalty, and what you operate to, which should sit above it so a miss opens a review instead of triggering a refund. The ceiling is not yours to pick either, because a request crossing four components in series is less available than the weakest of them — so either buy the difference with independent failure domains or publish a promise your own suppliers can support. The bill on all of this is a commitment made under uncertainty, and it is paid in two moves. Decide up front which behaviour may be dropped to protect the number, so degradation is a design decision rather than a judgement call by whoever is on call. Then re-price the figures whenever the business re-plans, in both directions: a target unquestioned since launch is either quietly failing or quietly overspending, and only the first one files a ticket.

Why it helps

A target is what makes an architecture arguable. “Highly available” cannot be designed to and cannot be tested against; 99.9% is a budget of about nine hours of downtime a year, and you can hold a design review about whether deploys, dependency failures and one bad night fit inside it. Every further nine divides that budget by ten and has to be bought with machinery — more copies in more failure domains, automatic failover instead of a person with a runbook — so the target sentence decides more of the system's cost than any other line in the document.

Recovery is two separate numbers, and each one buys a different piece of the design. How long you may take to come back settles whether standby capacity runs warm and whether failover is automatic or a phone call. How much data you may lose settles the replication mode, because asynchronous Replication is cheap and fast and by definition discards whatever had not reached the follower when the primary died, while making that number zero means paying a round trip on every write for the rest of the system's life. Both questions are cheap to answer in a planning meeting and expensive to answer after the storage engine is chosen.

Applying it

Get each number written down with an owner's name beside it:

  • Turn every “must be reliable” into a percentage over a stated window, then convert it to minutes and read the minutes back to whoever asked. A target nobody will recognise once it is expressed as downtime is a target nobody has actually agreed to.
  • Ask the two recovery questions before you choose a database: how long may we be down, and how much may we lose. Those answers pick the backup schedule, the replication mode and the failover design, so asking them afterwards turns them into a rebuild.
  • Keep what you promise and what you aim for as two different numbers. The commitment published to a customer carries a penalty and belongs below the internal goal you operate to, so that missing the goal opens a review instead of triggering a refund.
  • Add up your dependencies before signing anything. A request that crosses four components in series is less available than the weakest of them, so a promise above what your own suppliers commit to is one you are funding yourself out of extra copies and independent failure domains — or not funding at all.
  • State the cost ceiling the way you state the latency one. What the system may cost per month, or per thousand requests, is a requirement with a budget owner, and a design that hits every other number at four times the budget has failed a requirement rather than exceeded one.
  • Decide in advance which behaviour may disappear to protect the number. If checkout must hold its target and recommendations need not, Load Shedding the second to save the first is a business decision taken calmly in a design review rather than a judgement call taken at 3am by whoever is on call.
  • Draw the system's boundaries on the business's own divisions — what it sells, who owns each line, how each is priced. When the business reorganises what it sells, a system cut that way follows with a change per division, while one cut along technical layers has to be re-sliced across all of them.

A System Design Interview opens with exactly this move, for exactly this reason: no box on the diagram can be defended until the numbers it is answering exist. Outside the interview the only difference is that the numbers have an owner you can go and ask.

Taken too far

The first way this goes wrong is a target chosen by ambition. Four nines picked because it sounded appropriately serious is a commitment to under five minutes of downtime a month, which rules out manual recovery, most maintenance windows and any release that cannot roll itself back — and nobody who picked the number was shown that bill. Recovery objectives copied from a template fail the same way: “zero data loss” written into a document by someone who never priced it buys synchronous replication and the write latency that comes with it, on every write, forever.

The second is using the business as a trump card. “The business asked for it” ends a conversation engineering was supposed to have — the one where you say what the request costs, what it rules out, and what the next cheapest option would give up in exchange. A stakeholder naming a figure is making a request, and it becomes a requirement only once they have seen the price and still want it. Passing the figure straight through to a design is an abdication wearing customer focus as a costume.

The third is treating the numbers as settled. They were priced against a business with a particular traffic shape, customer mix and tolerance for a bad hour, and each of those moves: the tier that once justified cross-region failover becomes 2% of revenue, and the batch job that used to have all night now feeds a screen someone watches. Re-derive the figures on whatever cadence the business re-plans on, and be as willing to lower a target as to raise one — an over-served requirement is money that was never asked for, and unlike a missed target it never files a ticket.
