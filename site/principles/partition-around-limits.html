Principle

Partition Around Limits

Everything you build on has a ceiling — rows per table, connections per pool, requests per account, messages per queue, cores per quota. Find the one you will reach first, then divide the work so that no single unit of it ever gets close, and so that the unit which does fails alone.

Principle

What it says

Divide the system so that no single unit of it approaches the ceiling of anything it runs on. That means naming the ceiling first — the specific number, the current value, and the distance between them — and only then choosing where the line goes. Splitting is not the point; staying clear of a known limit is the point, and a split aimed at the wrong ceiling costs you the complexity and buys you nothing. This is also not a rule about databases. Databases are simply the layer whose limits get discussed.

You inherit every ceiling of every layer beneath you, and they are nested rather than parallel: rows and index depth per table, connections per pool, file handles per process, requests per second per account, instances per group, messages per second per queue, cores per quota in a region. Only one of them binds at a time, and the one that binds first is rarely the one anyone planned for — teams model storage growth for a year and then run out of connections in a month. Find the binding ceiling by measuring the distance to each, not by picking the one that is most interesting to solve.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Everything you build on runs out of something. A table gets too big to query quickly, a pool runs out of connections, an account runs out of its allowance for the day. If all the work sits in one place, the day you reach that number the whole product stops, and there is no quick fix because the number is not yours to change. Split the work into pieces small enough that none of them ever gets close, and when one of them does reach its ceiling, it breaks by itself while the rest keep serving.

Advanced

The ceilings are nested and only one binds at a time: rows and index depth per table, connections per pool, requests per second per account, messages per queue, cores per quota. Measure the distance to each before you choose where the line goes, because a split aimed at the wrong ceiling buys nothing and still costs you cross-node joins forever. The key is then the whole decision. Hash a high-cardinality identifier, and stay away from values that cluster — surnames and timestamps both pile the current traffic into one piece, which moves the problem rather than solving it. Databases are only the obvious candidate; queues, caches, worker pools and accounts all partition on the same reasoning.

Expert

Partition at the lowest level in the hierarchy that clears the binding ceiling. A table is a migration you can run twice, a service is a deployment, and a whole account is a second copy of your operational surface — cost and irreversibility both climb as you go up, so buy the cheapest level that works. Three bills come with the decision, each with a counter-move. The key ossifies, since changing it rewrites every row, so choose one whose meaning survives the product changing. Crossings erase the gain, because a spanning query costs what the slowest partition costs and a spanning write is a distributed transaction, so build read models for the access paths that cannot carry the key. And operational load grows with the count, so keep the count as low as the ceiling permits, pick a scheme where adding a partition moves few keys, and rehearse the rebalance — an untested resplit path fails on the night you need it.

Why it helps

An unpartitioned unit has a fixed ceiling and you get one chance to be under it. A partitioned one has a ceiling per partition, so growth becomes an arithmetic problem you solve by adding partitions rather than a cliff you hit at an hour of someone else's choosing. The second gain is containment: a partition that exhausts its own limit exhausts it alone, which turns a total outage into a fraction of customers seeing errors while everyone else keeps working — the same argument that makes a Bulkhead worth its overhead.

Partitioning also makes capacity knowable, which is worth as much as the capacity itself. A bounded unit can be load-tested to its own ceiling, and once you know what one partition holds you can multiply, plan the next split against a growth rate, and set an alert at a percentage of a number you actually know. An unbounded shared unit gives you no such figure: you learn where its limit was on the day you crossed it, from your customers, during an incident.

Applying it

Name the ceiling first, and only then choose where the line goes:

  • Write down the limit you are partitioning around, with its number and your current distance from it. Splitting a table across ten nodes does nothing for a per-account request quota that all ten still count against, and you will not discover that until the split is done and the errors are unchanged. If nobody can state the ceiling, the partition scheme is guesswork with a migration attached.
  • Pick the key on how traffic distributes, not on what is convenient to read. A key that concentrates load has moved the problem rather than solved it, and the result is a Hot Partition doing most of the work while its peers idle. Hash a high-cardinality identifier, and never partition on anything whose distribution follows natural language or the clock — surnames and timestamps both pile today's traffic into one place.
  • Partition at the lowest level that solves the problem. The hierarchy runs from a table, through a database, a service and a cluster, up to a whole account, and every step up multiplies what you operate while making the decision harder to reverse. Splitting one table is a migration you can run twice; splitting one account is a second copy of everything you run, including the parts that were fine.
  • Remember that queues, caches, pools and accounts partition too. A shared worker pool where one tenant's slow requests starve everyone else is a Noisy Neighbour problem with a partitioning answer: separate pools, or a per-tenant cap enforced by a Rate Limiter, so one caller's appetite meets a limit that is theirs alone.
  • Carry the partition key with the work. A request that arrives without enough information to name its partition must be sent to all of them, and its latency becomes whatever the slowest one costs. Put the key in the path, the message header or the token, and design the queries that cannot carry it as their own read model rather than as a scan.
  • Choose a scheme that survives resizing, and rehearse the resize. Splitting by a modulus of the partition count moves nearly every key when the count changes, which is why Consistent Hashing or an explicit range map is worth the extra indirection — the first split you make is never the last. A rebalance path you have never executed is a rebalance path that does not work.
  • Let the domain draw the line where it already has one. A Bounded Context that owns its data splits schema and load in the same cut, and that boundary holds through reorganisations because it follows meaning rather than a hash. Where the domain offers no such seam, Sharding on a synthetic key is the honest fallback.

Two questions decide the whole design: which ceiling binds first, and which key spreads load evenly beneath it. A partitioning plan that cannot answer both is complexity you will maintain for no return.

Taken too far

Partitioning before you are near any ceiling pays the whole cost up front for a benefit that arrives on a date you invented. From the first day you write joins that span nodes, transactions that cannot span them, and tooling that has to know which unit holds what — to stay clear of a limit that is two years or two orders of magnitude away, and may never arrive at all. One machine holds far more than most teams estimate, and the estimate is the cheap thing to check first.

The line, once drawn, is the expensive part to move. A key you cannot change without rewriting every row commits you to a distribution you chose before you had traffic to observe, so pick one whose meaning will still be true after the product changes. Watch the crossings, too: a query that spans partitions costs whatever the slowest partition costs, and a write that spans two is a distributed transaction carrying all the agreement you split the data to avoid. Once a meaningful share of your operations cross the boundary, the boundary is in the wrong place, and correcting it moves data rather than configuration.

Operational cost then grows with the count, close to linearly and with no natural stopping point. Every partition is another thing to back up, restore, patch, monitor, fail over and rebalance, and the shell script that handles four of them is a platform team at four hundred — so treat partition count as a number to keep as low as the binding ceiling permits, not as a score. The ceiling itself can also move: a quota someone else sets can be raised on request or lowered on their schedule, and a storage engine's practical limit shifts with a version upgrade. Re-measure before the next split, because a partition scheme built around last year's number is complexity you are now maintaining for a constraint that no longer exists.
