Cloud capability

Messaging & Eventing

Four shapes of managed messaging — queue, topic, event bus and stream — what each one is actually for, what every cloud calls it, and why a queue is consumed while a stream is only read.

Capability

What the cloud gives you here

Every cloud sells the same job here: a durable place to put a message, so the sender and the receiver stop having to be healthy at the same instant. It arrives in four shapes. A queue holds work for whichever consumer takes it next, a topic copies each message to every subscriber, an event bus puts routing rules in front of a topic, and a stream keeps an ordered log that readers move through at their own pace.

A queue is consumed and a stream is read, and that is the one distinction here you cannot paper over afterwards. A queue message is deleted when a consumer acknowledges it, so a queue holds work; a stream leaves the record where it is and tracks an offset per reader, so a stream holds history and a consumer written next month can replay from the beginning. Choosing the wrong one is not a performance mistake — it is the afternoon you discover you cannot rebuild a projection, because the messages that built it were acknowledged away.

What differs in kind between providers is not the vocabulary, it is where the guarantee stops. All of these services deliver at least once, so a duplicate is ordinary traffic rather than an incident, and idempotency stays your job however managed the broker is. Ordering, deduplication and dead-lettering are each a mode you switch on, and each carries a ceiling or a bill that differs enough between clouds to change the design.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Your checkout endpoint charges the card, then calls the receipt service, then the stock service, then analytics. The customer waits for all four, and when the receipt service is down the sale fails for a reason the customer has nothing to do with. The cloud sells you somewhere to leave a note instead: checkout writes down that an order happened, answers the customer, and the other services pick the note up when they are ready. The note is written to disk before anybody is asked to act on it, so a restart in the middle costs you nothing.

Advanced

The place you leave the note comes in four shapes, and swapping one for another later is a rewrite. A queue holds work items and deletes each one the moment a consumer acknowledges it, so ten workers on one queue divide the backlog between them. A topic copies every message to every subscription, so a new subscriber adds a delivery rather than taking a share. An event bus is a topic with a matching engine in front of it: rules read the message body and pick the targets, which turns adding a consumer into configuration instead of a publisher deploy. A stream keeps records in an ordered log for a fixed retention window and gives each reader its own position, which is what makes replay possible at all. All four deliver at least once, and ordering, where it is offered, holds inside a partition or a session and never across the whole service.

Expert

Choose on one question first: will a reader you have not met yet need these records? If it might, the answer is a stream, because retention plus per-reader offsets is the only arrangement that answers it, and the retention window is the hard outer edge of your replay. Then pay the bill. At-least-once delivery means duplicates, and the counter-move is a deduplication key your consumer checks against a store you own, because a broker's own dedup window runs for minutes while your retries and replays do not. Ordered modes carry a throughput ceiling and usually a separate resource type, so scope the ordering requirement to a key and never take it globally. Fan-out is billed per delivery, so one event on twenty subscriptions costs twenty times one — collapse it into a single stream with independent readers wherever the consumers can pull. And no broker joins your database transaction: write the message into your own database in the same commit and relay it from there, or accept that a crash between the two leaves your state and your events disagreeing.

The capabilities

Point-to-point queue
A durable line of work items where each message goes to exactly one consumer and is deleted when that consumer acknowledges it. Reach for it when the message is a task with an owner: adding consumers divides the backlog rather than duplicating the work.
Publish-subscribe topic
A named channel that copies every published message to every subscription attached to it. Reach for it when the message is an announcement rather than a task, and the publisher must not learn who is listening.
Event bus with content routing
A topic with a matching engine in front of it: rules read fields inside the message and decide which targets receive it. It turns wiring a new consumer into a configuration change instead of a deploy of the publisher, and it is where the provider's own events — a file landed, a machine started — are published for you to subscribe to.
Append-only stream with replay
An ordered log of records, partitioned for throughput and retained for a fixed window, where every reader tracks its own position. Reach for it when a second reader may appear later, because the record survives being read and a consumer built next month can start from the beginning.
Lease-based redelivery
The broker hands a message to one consumer and hides it for a lease; when no acknowledgement arrives before the lease expires, the message returns to the queue. This is where at-least-once comes from, and why a consumer that finishes its work and then dies will see the same message a second time.
Dead-letter destination
A separate queue or topic that receives a message once delivery has failed a set number of times. It stops one poison message from blocking the consumer forever, and it puts the failure somewhere you can read it.
Subscription filters
A predicate attached to a subscription so the broker delivers only the messages that match it. It moves the if-statement out of your consumer and into the broker, which cuts both the delivery bill and the wasted invocations.
Ordering and deduplication modes
An opt-in mode that delivers messages in order within one key, partition or session, and discards a republished duplicate inside a short window. Both are scoped rather than absolute: order holds inside a partition and never across the service, and the deduplication window is measured in minutes.
Delayed and scheduled delivery
Publishing a message now that becomes visible to consumers only after a delay or at a chosen time. It covers retry backoff, reminders and anything else where the work is known but not yet due, with no scheduler of your own to run.
Managed Kafka
The Kafka protocol operated for you, so existing Kafka clients, connectors and stream-processing jobs keep working unchanged. Reach for it when your portability requirement is the client library rather than the service, and accept that the topic and partition layout stays your problem.
Stateful workflow orchestration
A managed state machine that calls services in order, waits, retries and runs compensating steps, with the position of every run persisted for you. Reach for it when the coordination itself has to survive a restart — it is where a saga becomes a definition the provider runs rather than retry code in your service.
Device-facing MQTT broker
A broker speaking MQTT over long-lived connections, sized for many small, intermittently connected clients rather than a few servers. It carries a per-device identity and per-device credentials, which is the part you would otherwise build and rotate yourself.

Choosing between them

Ask one question before you read a single product name: will a reader you have not met yet need these records? If it might, you need a stream, because retention plus a per-reader offset is the only arrangement that lets a consumer written later start from the beginning. If it will not, ask the second question instead — is this message a task with exactly one owner, or an announcement with an audience?

The broker gives you at-least-once and you still owe idempotency. A consumer that completes its work and dies before acknowledging will be handed the same message again, and buying the queue instead of running it moves that redelivery into someone else's operations rather than out of your data. Give every message a stable key, record the keys you have already applied, and check that record before you act — a broker's own deduplication window runs for minutes, and your retries, replays and redeployments do not respect it.

The adjacent debt is the write. No broker joins your database transaction, so a state change plus a publish is two operations that can disagree: commit the row, crash before the publish, and nothing downstream ever hears about it. Write the event into your own database in the same commit and relay it from there — that is the outbox, and this capability is the clearest case on the site of a managed service that implements several patterns for you and leaves the adjacent one entirely yours.

Then watch the depth. A managed queue will accept far more than your consumers can drain and bill you for the storage, so the buffer that was protecting you becomes an unbounded queue with an hour of latency in it; alarm on queue depth and oldest-message age, not on error rate, and apply backpressure at the producer when depth stops falling. The drain is the other half: a backlog released after an outage arrives as fast as your consumers can pull it, which is a thundering herd aimed at whatever those consumers call next.

What does not port

  • Maximum message size differs by an order of magnitude: one service's ceiling is measured in hundreds of kilobytes and another's in megabytes, so a payload that publishes today can fail after a port with nothing else changed. Put the body in object storage and send the key — the claim check is not an optimisation here, it is what keeps the design movable.
  • Ordering is a mode with a ceiling, not a flag: ordered and session-based delivery mean a distinct queue type or an ordering key set at publish time, and they carry throughput limits the unordered path does not have. Turning one on for an existing deployment is often a new resource and a cutover rather than a setting you flip.
  • Retention sets your maximum replay: a stream keeps records for a window measured in days, and extending it is a billed option rather than a free setting. That number is your replay horizon, so pick it deliberately and land a copy in object storage if you need history beyond it.
  • Dead-lettering is automatic on some services and wiring on others: some brokers give you the dead-letter destination and the retry count by default, while elsewhere you create the target and attach a policy, and a queue with no policy retries a poison message forever. Check which one you have before the first poison message, because otherwise you find out from a stuck consumer.
  • Lease semantics and in-flight limits differ: how many messages one consumer may hold unacknowledged, how long the lease lasts, and whether you can extend it mid-job all vary. A long job under a short lease is the classic import — the message is redelivered while the first consumer is still working, and the work runs twice.
  • Fan-out is billed per delivery: per-message pricing multiplies by subscriber count, so one event across twenty subscriptions is twenty billable deliveries and twenty invocations. Where consumers can pull instead, one stream with independent readers costs a fraction of the same fan-out expressed as subscriptions.
  • One provider's topic is another's stream: some clouds sell publish-subscribe and streaming as two services with different pricing and different replay behaviour, and others fold both into one. Map on the two properties that decide your design — is the record deleted on acknowledgement, and does each reader keep an offset — never on the name.
  • Exactly-once is always scoped: where a provider offers it, the guarantee holds inside a window, a partition or a single subscription, and it says nothing about the side effects your consumer performs against other systems. Read the scope, then write the consumer as though the guarantee were not there.
