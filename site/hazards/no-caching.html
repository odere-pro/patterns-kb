Hazard

No Caching

Every request pays the full price of an answer that has not changed since the last request asked for it — the same row read, the same remote call made, the same result computed, thousands of times over. Nothing is wrong with any single request; the waste is only visible in the aggregate.

Hazard

What it is

No caching is a system that recomputes or re-fetches the same unchanged answer for every request that asks for it. The reference table read on every page load, the remote profile call made once per request for a user who visits forty times an hour, the expensive derivation recalculated from identical inputs — each one is correct, and each one is work already done. Under light traffic the omission is invisible, which is exactly why it survives into the load where it matters: the cost is the repetition, and repetition only arrives with concurrency.

Its signature is a workload that is overwhelmingly reads of a small, popular subset. Query statistics show one statement executed orders of magnitude more often than any other; the dependency's traffic tracks your request rate exactly, one call per request, rather than tracking how often the underlying data changes. That last ratio is the diagnostic — when reads outnumber writes by a wide margin and the read path still touches the source every time, the source is being asked a question whose answer it already gave.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Ten thousand people open the same front page in an hour, and the system works the answer out ten thousand times, though it did not change once. Every one of them waits for the full trip to the database, and the database spends its day answering a question it has already answered. Keep the answer close by for a minute and almost all of those trips disappear. The catch is the obvious one: for that minute you may be showing something slightly out of date, so this is for the things where being a minute behind is fine, and not for the things where it is not.

Advanced

Two numbers decide it — how often a key is read compared with how often it changes, and how much lag the reader tolerates. Where an answer is served far more often than it moves, the source is being asked a settled question, and load on it grows with audience size instead of with how often the truth changes, which is the worst direction available. The mechanism is a lookup placed in front of the source, filled either by the caller on a miss or by the cache itself, with an expiry that bounds how wrong the copy may be. Where that copy lives sets both the saving and the exposure: in the process, in a shared tier, at the edge, or on the device that asked.

Expert

Price the three new failure modes on the way in, because each has a specific counter. A copy can be stale, and the counter is an expiry derived from what the reader tolerates rather than from what feels safe. A popular entry expiring under load sends every reader to the source at once, and the counter is collapsing the concurrent misses into a single load, or refreshing ahead of expiry rather than after it. An unreachable cache has to fall back to the source, and the counter is a limit on how much of that traffic is admitted, since a cold cache in front of a busy source is the outage you built it to prevent. Then expect the bottleneck to move: relieving the store commonly reveals a tier in front of it that was never sized for the throughput it can now sustain, and that is the next thing to scale, not evidence the cache was a mistake.

How it happens

  • Not caching is simpler, and works. A direct read is one line, always correct, and adequate at the load the system had when it was written. A cache adds a second copy of the truth and a question about how long it may lag — real complexity, in exchange for a benefit nobody is feeling yet.
  • Correctness anxiety wins the argument. The fear of serving something out of date is concrete and easy to picture, while the cost of not caching is diffuse and shows up as a bill or a saturation curve. So the safe-sounding choice is made without pricing the alternative.
  • The environment changed underneath the design. Code carried over from a setting where the data was local and the hardware was generous keeps assuming a cheap read after the store has moved across a network and behind a quota.
  • The available caching layer goes unnoticed. Caching is thought of as a server the team must run, so the ones that need no server — validators and freshness headers on an HTTP response, an edge tier already in the path, the process's own memory — are never considered.
  • Nobody knows the read-to-write ratio. Without instrumentation that says which answers are requested repeatedly and how rarely they change, the case for a cache cannot be made with a number, so it is made with an opinion and loses.

What it costs

  • Every reader pays the slowest path. Response time includes the full round-trip to the source on every single request, so the floor under your latency is the source's latency — no matter how often the answer was identical.
  • The source scales with traffic instead of with change. Load on the store or the dependency is a function of how many people ask, not of how often the answer moves, so growth in readers turns directly into growth in a resource that is usually the hardest one to add.
  • Contention arrives before capacity does. Repeated reads occupy connections, locks and buffer space that concurrent writes need, so the write path slows down because of read volume it has nothing to do with.
  • Metered and quota-bound dependencies punish it directly. Where a downstream service charges per call or throttles past a rate, uncached repetition converts into money and into rejections that look like the dependency failing.
  • The ceiling is lower than the hardware suggests. Throughput plateaus at the source's limit while the tier in front of it still has capacity to spare, so scaling out the part you can scale buys nothing, and the system looks saturated well below what it cost.

Getting out

Start with what the data does rather than with a cache product. Answers that are read far more often than they change, and whose readers can live with being a little behind, are the candidates; everything else is not. The usual first move is Cache-Aside — look in the cache, and on a miss fetch from the source and put the value there for the next reader — which keeps the source of truth exactly where it was and leaves the read path obviously correct on a cold start. Where you would rather the caller not see the miss at all, Read-Through moves that fetch behind the cache's own interface, at the cost of a component that now needs to know how to load.

Then decide where the copy lives, because that choice sets both the saving and the exposure. Keeping it in the process is the fastest possible hit and gives every instance its own copy to keep consistent; a shared cache tier trades a network hop for one answer the whole fleet agrees on; a CDN or a Client-Side Cache stops the request before it reaches you at all, which is the largest saving available and the hardest to invalidate once it is out there. Caching partially is legitimate: hold the static bulk of an object and fetch only the field that moves.

Adding a cache installs new failure modes, so adopt their counters at the same time. A second copy of the truth can be wrong — that is Stale Cache, and it is bounded by an expiry chosen from what the reader tolerates, not from what feels safe. A popular entry expiring under load sends every reader to the source at once — that is a Cache Stampede, countered by collapsing the concurrent misses into one load or refreshing before expiry. And an unreachable cache must degrade to the source rather than to an error, with a limit on how much of that traffic is allowed through, since a cold cache in front of a busy source is the outage the cache existed to prevent.

Instrument the thing before and after. Hit rate, and the ratio of reads to writes per key, are what tell you whether an entry earns its place and what its expiry should be; without them, expiries get tuned by anecdote and the cache slowly becomes a second database nobody trusts. Expect the bottleneck to move rather than disappear — relieving the store commonly reveals that the tier in front of it was never sized for the throughput it can now sustain, and that is the next thing to scale, not evidence the cache was wrong.
