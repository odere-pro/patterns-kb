Hazard

Starvation

A fairness failure: one class of work is perpetually overtaken and never reaches the front, while throughput, error rate and the machine all look healthy.

Hazard

What it is

Starvation is work that never gets its turn. A share of the requests, threads or messages is perpetually overtaken by others, so it waits without bound while everything around it is served normally. Nothing has failed and nothing is blocked — the victim simply never reaches the front.

That is what separates it from deadlock, and the difference decides how you find it. Under deadlock nobody progresses, so the freeze is its own alarm. Under starvation the system progresses fine: throughput sits at target, the workers stay busy, and one class of work never completes.

It is not a race condition either. A race does the wrong thing quickly; starvation does the right thing for everyone except one class, indefinitely. What is broken is fairness, not correctness — the allocation policy is doing exactly what you configured, and what you configured put no floor under anybody.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Every job waiting for the same workers sits in one line, and the ones marked urgent are allowed to jump ahead. That works until urgent jobs keep arriving. A worker finishes one urgent job, finds another already waiting, and takes that instead, so the job at the back is passed over again and again and may never run at all. Nothing looks wrong while this happens: the workers are busy, jobs are completing, and nobody is reporting an error. The way out is to promise the back of the line something — after waiting long enough it moves up, or a worker is held back just for it.

Advanced

Three mechanisms produce it, and each one is a policy somebody chose. A strictly preemptive scheduler serves the low class only when the high class is empty, which stops happening once high-priority arrivals exceed the drain rate. A lock that admits every arriving reader while readers are present, or that lets a just-released thread barge back in ahead of the queued waiter, passes over that waiter on every round — unfairness is the default because it is faster. And a share of a partitioned resource sized below its class's arrival rate starves that class inside the compartment built to protect it. In all three the queue is stable in aggregate and unbounded for one class, which is why the aggregate number tells you nothing.

Expert

Detect it on per-class quantities or not at all: tail latency broken out by class, the age of the oldest item still queued, and a queue-time histogram rather than a mean, because a minority class waiting forever barely moves an average. Then price the fixes. Aging bounds the worst case and gives back part of the strict-priority guarantee you paid for, since an aged low item now delays urgent work. Reserved floors bound it too and charge you utilisation, because the reserved slice idles whenever its class is quiet. Fair queueing removes it by construction at the cost of bookkeeping and peak throughput — the barging that starves a waiter is the same thing that makes an unfair lock fast. A bounded wait serves none of the starved work but converts an invisible failure into a visible error, which makes it the cheapest thing to ship first and the fastest way to learn how bad it already is.

How it happens

The setup is always the same three parts: a shared server — a thread pool, a lock, a set of consumers — a policy that decides who goes next, and a class of arrivals the policy keeps preferring. Starvation begins the moment the preferred class arrives faster than the server drains it, because "when the preferred class is idle" then never comes. These setups recur:

  • Strict priority under sustained load. The low class runs only when the high class is empty, and under a steady stream of urgent work it never is. A priority queue draining highest-first asks for exactly this preemption; starvation is the price of the guarantee.
  • Lock preference policy. A read-write lock that admits every arriving reader while readers are present holds the writer out for as long as the read traffic lasts. Flip the preference to writers and the readers starve instead.
  • Unfair or barging locks. A thread that has just released a lock or a semaphore permit re-acquires it before the queued waiter finishes waking, so the waiter is passed over on every round. Barging is faster than fairness, which is why most primitives are unfair by default.
  • An undersized share of a partitioned resource. Split the pool per class and each class waits only on its own capacity — but size one compartment below its arrival rate and its queue grows without bound inside the wall you built to protect it. As bulkhead puts it, undersize a compartment and you have rebuilt the starvation you meant to prevent.
  • Retry amplification. A client that times out and retries multiplies its own share of the server, so the loudest caller's second attempt crowds out the quiet caller's first. The quiet caller then times out and joins in, and the imbalance widens itself.

What it costs

The first cost is that your instruments cannot see it. Throughput holds at target, the workers stay busy, and the error rate is zero, so every alarm that watches errors stays green while the victim's latency grows without bound. The machine looks healthy because it is healthy; what is failing is the order it serves people in, and no host metric records that.

Averages hide it too. The starved class is a minority of the traffic by definition, so a mean or a median barely moves while one class waits forever. It shows up in the tail, in the age of the oldest item still queued, and in latency broken out per class — or it does not show up at all. Until someone charts those three, your first report of the incident is a customer telling you about it.

Then comes the business cost: work that is silently never done. You meet the aggregate service-level target and miss it for one class, so the number you report and the experience you sold disagree. The backlog drains at a healthy rate while its oldest entries never move. And the low-priority job somebody marked deferrable turns out to have had a deadline nobody wrote down — a password reset, a refund, a compliance export.

Getting out

Give the starved class a guarantee, and expect to pay for it. Each counter-move below hands back some of the preference you originally asked for:

  • Aging. Raise an item's priority as it waits, so a long enough wait eventually wins. This is the standard answer and it bounds the worst case; it costs you part of the strict-priority guarantee, because a sufficiently aged low item now delays urgent work.
  • Reserved floors. Guarantee each class a slice of capacity — one worker held back, a fixed share of the pool — so its worst case is bounded rather than unbounded. You pay in utilisation, since the reserved slice idles whenever its class is quiet.
  • Fair or ticket-based queueing. Serve in arrival order and no policy can prefer anyone, which removes starvation by construction. It costs bookkeeping and some peak throughput: the barging that starves a waiter is the same thing that makes an unfair lock fast. Scheduling is where you choose between the two.
  • Bounded waits. A timed acquire or a queue-time deadline turns an unbounded wait into an error. It does not serve the starved work — it makes the starvation visible, which is worth more than it sounds, because it puts the failure onto the error-rate dashboard that was staying green.
  • Isolation into separate pools. Give each class its own workers and one class can no longer take another's. Size every compartment against its own arrival rate: undersize one and you have rebuilt the starvation inside the wall.

Instrument before you tune. Per-class tail latency and the age of the oldest queued item tell you whether the policy still has a floor under it; without both, every fix above is a change you cannot check.
