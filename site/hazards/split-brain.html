Hazard

Split-Brain

A network partition cuts a cluster in two and both halves conclude the other is dead, so both elect a leader and both accept writes — leaving two divergent versions of the truth that no later merge can fully reconcile.

Hazard

What it is

Split brain is a cluster that has divided into two groups, each of which believes it is the surviving whole. The link between them fails, neither can hear the other, and both apply the same rule they were built to apply when a leader disappears: elect a new one and carry on. Now two nodes hold the leader role at the same time, both accept writes, and the single copy of the truth the cluster was designed around has quietly become two.

The trap is that a lost message is indistinguishable from a dead peer. A node that stops answering may be crashed, or paused by a long garbage-collection cycle, or perfectly healthy behind a broken switch — and from the outside those look identical. Waiting forever means the cluster stalls whenever one machine hiccups; promoting immediately means promoting a second leader while the first is still serving. That choice is not a bug to be fixed but the availability-versus-consistency trade taken explicitly.

You recognize it after the fact, in the reconciliation. The network heals, the two halves rejoin, and rows that should have one value have two — counters that both incremented from the same base, an order marked shipped on one side and cancelled on the other, two clients each holding what they were told was an exclusive lock. Monitoring rarely catches it while it runs, because each half reports itself healthy and serving; the alarm that does catch it is two nodes claiming the leader role in the same term.

Explained at three levels

Basic

A cable fails between two rooms of machines that were meant to act as one system. Each room can still hear itself but not the other, and each concludes the other side is dead — so each picks its own boss and keeps taking work. For a while everything looks fine: both sides answer requests, both sides look healthy. The damage appears when the cable is fixed and the two sides compare notes, because the same account was changed in two different ways and nobody can say which one really happened. The way to prevent it is a rule agreed up front: a side may only keep working if it can still see more than half the machines, and the smaller side has to stop.

Advanced

A network partition plus promotion on timeout gives two leaders, because a failure detector cannot separate a crashed peer from an unreachable or paused one. Both sides then accept writes against the same keys, and the histories diverge until the partition heals — at which point a merge has to discard somebody's committed work. Quorum removes the first half of that: require a strict majority of voting members to elect and to commit, and at most one side of any partition can qualify. Fencing removes the second half: carry a monotonically increasing term token on every write and have the resource reject anything below the highest token it has seen, so a leader returning from a long pause is refused rather than applied. Cluster size and voter placement matter as much as the protocol — an even membership, or two sites with equal votes, has no majority to find.

Expert

Choose deliberately which side of the partition trade this workload sits on, because both answers are defensible and only the unexamined one produces split brain. Ledgers, inventory and anything a customer disputes belong on the consistent side: the minority stops writing, you take a bounded partial outage, and there is nothing to reconcile afterwards. Session state, telemetry and carts can stay available on both sides, but then the merge is a design deliverable — commutative or conflict-free structures, or a written resolution policy — and last-write-wins on wall-clock timestamps is a silent data-loss policy wearing a merge strategy's clothes. Price the bill honestly: quorum costs a round trip to a majority on every commit and turns the loss of half the cluster into an outage by design, while fencing costs a version check on the resource and a leadership term threaded through every write path. The failure detector is where the residual risk lives, since the same timeout that shortens failover also converts a garbage-collection pause into a spurious one — so watch elections per hour and the term number, treat repeated leadership changes as an incident in their own right, and rehearse the partition before it rehearses you.

How it happens

It starts with silence. One machine stops answering the others, and nobody can tell whether it died or the wire to it did. If the rule is "when the boss goes quiet, pick a new boss", then a broken wire between two rooms full of machines produces a boss in each room — and both of them start giving orders. The moves below are the ordinary ways a cluster ends up with that rule and no way to stop the second boss.

The root is that a partition is not observable from inside a partition: a timeout distinguishes nothing between a crashed peer, a paused peer and an unreachable one. Promotion on timeout alone therefore lets both sides promote, because each sees only its own side and a failure detector that says the rest is gone. Everything below is a variation on making that promotion possible without proof that the old leader has stopped.

Underneath sits the impossibility result the design has to respect: with asynchronous messaging you cannot both guarantee progress and guarantee agreement in the presence of failures, so a cluster must give one of them up on purpose. Split brain is what happens when nobody made that choice explicitly — availability was assumed and consistency was assumed, so the failure detector was tuned for the former and the write path was written for the latter. The causes below are all places where those two assumptions meet.

  • Promotion on timeout with no quorum requirement: any side that stops hearing the leader elects its own, so a two-way partition produces two leaders and a three-way one can produce three.
  • An even-sized cluster, or two data centres with equal votes, where no side can ever hold a strict majority — so either both sides promote, or neither does and the cluster stops.
  • A leader that was never actually stopped: a long garbage-collection pause or a hung disk makes it miss heartbeats, the cluster replaces it, and then it resumes still believing it holds the role.
  • A demoted leader whose in-flight writes are still accepted downstream, because the storage layer takes writes from whoever asks and has no way to tell that this caller's term has expired.
  • An asymmetric or partial partition, where A can reach B but B cannot reach A, so the two sides disagree about who is reachable and a failure detector on either side produces a different answer.

What it costs

  • Two versions of the truth, and no way to compute the right one. Both halves accepted valid writes, so the merge is not a technical question but a business one: someone has to decide which customer's order survives.
  • Silent damage while it runs. Each side answers requests successfully and reports itself healthy, so the incident starts at the moment of the partition and is discovered at the moment of the merge — often hours later.
  • Mutual exclusion stops holding. A distributed lock or a leader-only job granted on one side is granted again on the other, so two processes run a supposedly single-writer task and corrupt what it touches.
  • Recovery costs more than the outage would have. Reconciling divergent histories means comparing records, replaying logs and calling customers; refusing writes on the minority side for the same period would have been a bounded, understood loss of availability.
  • Downstream systems keep the damage. Both halves emitted events, sent notifications and charged cards; you can roll back a database row, but not an email or a payment already captured.

Price it against the alternative rather than against a healthy day. The counterfactual is not "no incident" but "the minority side rejects writes until the partition heals" — a partial outage with a known duration and no data to reconcile afterwards. Split brain converts that bounded availability loss into an unbounded correctness loss, and correctness losses do not expire when the network recovers: they surface as disputed invoices, duplicate shipments and a ledger that no longer reconciles months later. That asymmetry is why mature systems choose to stop rather than to guess, and why the argument for staying available during a partition has to be made per workload, not per cluster.

Getting out

Require a majority before anyone acts. Give the cluster an odd number of voting members and let only the side holding more than half of them elect a leader or accept a write; a partition can produce at most one majority, so the minority side stops instead of diverging. This is the whole point of the consensus protocols — a quorum turns "I cannot hear them" into "I do not have the right to proceed", which is the distinction a timeout alone can never make.

Then make an old leader harmless, because a quorum decides who may write and does not by itself stop who was writing. Fence the resource: attach a monotonically increasing token to the leadership term, have the storage or lock service record the highest token it has seen, and reject anything arriving with a lower one — so a leader that wakes up from a long pause finds its writes refused rather than applied. Where the resource cannot check a token, the blunt equivalent is to cut the old node off entirely — power it down or revoke its network access before promoting a replacement — so that exclusion is enforced by something outside the node's own judgment.

Choose the failure detector's timeouts with the cost of both mistakes in view: too short and a garbage-collection pause triggers a needless failover, too long and every real crash costs that much downtime. Where a majority is structurally impossible — two data centres with equal votes — add a small third voter in a third location whose only job is to break ties, rather than letting an even split decide by luck. And decide in advance what a workload does on the minority side: rejecting writes is the safe default, while accepting them is a deliberate choice that obliges you to design the merge, which means conflict-free data types or a recorded resolution policy, not a hope that timestamps will settle it.
