Case study

Persona Identification & Sanction Check (V2)

A long-running identity-and-sanctions flow whose system of record is the log itself: one append carries the state change, the auditor's evidence and the client's event, so no two of them can disagree.

Design

Understanding the problem

The task gives a flow and four external components — an identity-verification provider, several sanction lists, an email sender, and the client's own webhook endpoint. It gives no volumes, no jurisdictions and no data-protection rules. These questions close those gaps. Where no answer was available the assumption is marked, and every answer lands in a requirement below.

Q1 — How many person-flows a day, and how fast does that grow?
Assumed, not given: ~100 merchant onboardings a week and several people per merchant, so under 75 person-flows a day today, with headroom designed to 10k a day. Confirm this before reading any capacity number below — every verdict in Right-sizing is priced against it. → NFR: scale.

Q2 — How many sanction lists, and may a partial verdict ship?
Several, and no partial verdict ever reaches the client: a flow concludes only once every list its jurisdiction requires has reported. Assumed, not given: each vendor returns an adjudicated hit-or-clear rather than a fuzzy score, so no human review step lives inside this system. → FR: screening, fan-in; NFR: consistency.

Q3 — When does the screening obligation end?
When the client's relationship with the person ends, and the client has to tell us so. Without that signal the recurring book only grows: the system keeps paying to screen ex-customers, and keeps processing their personal data with no live lawful basis. → FR: relationship close, re-screening; NFR: scale.

Q4 — What must the history prove, and to whom?
An auditor asks what was checked, when, and on whose authority — about a flow that concluded years ago. The record has to be the one the system actually ran on, because a history written alongside the decision can disagree with it, and a history that can disagree is not evidence. → FR: history; NFR: consistency, compliance.

Q5 — What personal data is held, where may it live, and what must we produce on demand?

  • Name, date of birth and a photograph of a government identity document — personal data on people who are not our customers.
  • It stays in its client's region, and the region is fixed when the client is onboarded rather than chosen per request.
  • On demand: a flow's full history, and the record of who read the personal data behind it.
  • On demand: proof that data was erased, not an assurance that it was.
  • Retention is per jurisdiction and counted from the end of the relationship, not from the check — an expiry the system enforces, in permanent tension with erasure.
  • A cross-client leak is a reportable incident, not a bug.

→ FR: separate PII store, access audit, relationship close; NFR: compliance, security & tenancy.

Q6 — Store the identity photo, or pass it through?
Store it: the auditor's question is what was checked, and a system that only proxied the image cannot answer it. Storage is then sized by the retention clock rather than by traffic. → NFR: compliance; drives the storage estimate.

Q7 — Does the client want progress, and does the order of two results matter?
Only the result, and yes, order matters. A flow idles for days, so live progress would report almost nothing; but a re-screen can flip a verdict, and a stale clear arriving after a sanctioned hit is worse than no delivery at all. → FR: result delivery, verdict ordering; NFR: consistency.

Q8 — What happens when a flow gets stuck, how fast, and who hears about it?
Assumed, not given: an operator is paged within 15 minutes of a flow passing its state deadline, and the client is told within 5 minutes of the flow being declared stuck. Naming the numbers is the point — a service level nobody stated cannot be breached, so nothing ever escalates. → FR: failure notice; NFR: availability & resilience.

Q9 — Does residency mean multi-region, and does anything fail over? (open)
Residency is in scope — separate regional deployments sharing no data, at the cost of one copy of everything per region. Failover, meaning one client surviving the loss of their region, is not justified at this volume; it is for the client to say whether a regulator demands it. → NFR: security & tenancy; out of scope: geo-failover.

Q10 — What is deliberately not built?

  • Cancelling a flow in flight.
  • In-flight status polling — the dashboard answers that for humans.
  • Cross-region failover, per Q9.
  • Human review of a possible match, per Q2.

→ Out of scope.

Explained at three levels

Basic

A business must know that a new person is who they claim to be, and is not on a government blocked list. Build that the obvious way and three things break. The checkers can take days to answer, so a request left waiting on them ties up the server. If one list has answered and another has not, saying "clear" lets through someone who should have been stopped. And if you record the decision in one place but tell the customer from another, the two can end up saying different things. So write down what happened once, in a list that is only ever added to, and read the answer, the history and the message to the customer from that one list.

Advanced

Every change is one row appended to a per-flow log, and current state is a small table folded forward in the same transaction, so the two cannot disagree. A row's position in its flow does three jobs at once: it rejects a stale writer, it orders what the client is told, and it is the auditor's record. Stateless pools claim follow-up work from a task table with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, one pool per vendor behind shared circuit-breaker state; vendor callbacks re-enter through an inbox keyed on the sender's own request id; and a relay walks one flow's log at a time, so a stale verdict cannot overtake a fresh one. Personal data lives in its own encrypted vault, and erasure destroys a key rather than a row.

Expert

Choose this shape when the history is itself a deliverable and the flow idles for days: the audit obligation decides it, not the volumes, and the page is dishonest if it implies otherwise. At six writes a second one Postgres carries the log, the projections, the queue and the inbox, buying an atomicity a broker would trade away for throughput nobody needs. The bill is stated rather than hidden — the log is four contracts in one untyped payload column, a single writer means an outage stalls writes until the standby is promoted, and the log only grows. Each is paid: schema discipline on the payload, a standby that acknowledges before the client does, a time-partition exit at roughly 500 GB. The quantity that actually runs out first is none of those. Recurring screening compounds against the book of open relationships while revenue tracks only new ones, so the cheapest control on the page is the endpoint that ends the obligation.

Requirements

Functional

Mandatory — the product promise

  1. A client starts a flow via API or dashboard by providing an email address.
  2. A repeated create for the same open client-and-email pair returns the existing flow instead of starting a duplicate.
  3. The email's owner receives a single-use invitation link that expires after 48 hours.
  4. The person can request a fresh invitation link themselves if theirs expired.
  5. The submitted personal information and identity photo are verified through the given external identity-verification provider.
  6. Only after verification passes is the person screened against every sanction list their jurisdiction requires.
  7. A flow concludes only once every required list has reported for that screening round.
  8. The client receives the flow's result on a webhook.
  9. A result delivered more than once is recognisable to the client as a repeat of the same result.
  10. Two results for the same flow are recognisable to the client in the order the system produced them.

Additional — ongoing obligations and governance

  1. When a flow cannot proceed, the client is notified with a failure event.
  2. A concluded flow's sanction screening is re-run on a cadence set per jurisdiction.
  3. Identity is re-verified with a fresh document from the person when their document expires or is revoked.
  4. A verdict changed by re-screening or re-verification is delivered to the client the same way as the first result.
  5. A client can record that its relationship with a person has ended, and that ends the person's recurring obligations.
  6. Every state change is recorded, and any flow's full history can be reconstructed after the fact.
  7. A flow and its history can be viewed step by step in the dashboard.
  8. Personal data is kept in its own store, separate from the flow's operational data.
  9. Every access to personal data is recorded with who accessed it, when, and for what purpose.

Non-functional

  • Scale
    • ~100 merchant onboardings a week today; one merchant means several person-flows.
    • Headroom to 10k person-flows a day without redesign.
    • Recurring re-screening grows with the book of open relationships, not with daily intake, and must never starve live flows.
    • That book is bounded by relationships still open, not by everyone ever checked.
  • Consistency
    • The recorded history and what the client was told must never diverge.
    • Behaviour is asynchronous end to end; when speed and correctness pull apart, correctness wins.
    • A repeated or replayed input — a create, a vendor callback, a delivery — leaves the flow in the same state as its first arrival.
    • Results for one flow reach the client in the order the system produced them.
  • Availability & resilience
    • The identity-verification vendor is unavailable ~6 hours a week: queue and wait, never fail the flow for a vendor outage.
    • A flow past its state deadline raises an operator page within 15 minutes, and a client failure event within 5 minutes of being declared stuck.
    • Every error path is explicit: a failed step retries, escalates, or ends the flow with a recorded reason.
    • A database failover loses no acknowledged fact.
    • The system refuses new work rather than accepting work it cannot drain.
  • Observability
    • One flow id correlates every recorded fact, vendor call and delivery attempt.
    • Alarms fire on stuck flows, per-class queue age, per-vendor error rates and dead work.
    • Every alarm names what it means, how to diagnose it and how to recover, and that text ships with the alarm definition rather than in a wiki beside it.
  • Compliance
    • Verified documents are stored as evidence, not proxied.
    • Personal data is encrypted at rest; right-to-forget is honoured and provable.
    • Retention is a per-jurisdiction policy counted from the end of the relationship, enforced and evidenced by the system.
    • No identifier kept outside the personal-data store may be used to re-identify an erased person.
  • Security & tenancy
    • Tenant isolation is enforced by the database itself, not by application filters alone.
    • Internal services and data stores are reachable only over a private network, never from the public internet.
    • Webhooks are authenticated; invitation links cannot be reused.
    • Each client belongs to a region recorded at onboarding, and their data stays in it.
  • Evolvability
    • A deployment meeting only the mandatory requirements is a complete, correct product on its own.
    • Each additional obligation attaches to the recorded history of the mandatory flow without redesigning it.
    • A new jurisdiction, list or vendor changes configuration and cadences, not the shape of the system.

Out of scope

  • Cancelling a flow in flight — a started flow runs to a terminal or is abandoned.
  • Status polling in flight — recovery is webhook replay plus the dashboard, not a second read path.
  • Cross-region failover — regional isolation for residency is in scope; surviving the loss of a region is not.
  • Human review of a possible match — vendors return an adjudicated verdict, so no review state exists here.

Right-sizing

The problem: ~75 person-flows a day today (assumed: ~100 merchant onboardings a week, five people each), design target 10k a day, each waiting days on a person and hours on vendors. The shape: event-driven, and an append-only log rather than a mutated row — the waits force the first and the auditor forces the second, because a history written alongside the decision can disagree with it and a history that can disagree is not evidence. Current state is a projection maintained in the same transaction as the append, since folding years of a re-screened person's events on every dashboard read buys nothing the projection does not already give. The stores: five per region — operational Postgres (the event log, the projections, the task queue and the inbox) with a synchronous standby, a separate encrypted vault for personal data, a key manager holding envelope keys the application cannot export, object storage for documents, and a coordination cache holding breaker state, provider weights, rate-limit counters and the schedulers' leases. The deployment unit: residency means one full stack per region, so every number below is per region and the largest region dominates.

Required capabilities:

  • Durable transactional store — one append carries the state change, the auditor's evidence and the client's event, or none of them lands (mandatory). → NFR: consistency.
  • Append-only history as the system of record — the auditor's question is answered from the record the system ran on (mandatory). → FR: history; NFR: compliance.
  • Work queue — the vendor is down ~6 hours a week and the person takes about a day, so every step must be able to wait (mandatory). → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Per-flow ordered delivery — a re-screen can flip a verdict, and a stale clear must never overtake a hit (mandatory). → FR: verdict ordering; NFR: consistency.
  • Coordination cache — one breaker record, one weight set and one rate counter per vendor shared by every replica, plus the schedulers' leases (mandatory). → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Rate limiting, inbound and outbound — vendor quota is finite, and the self-serve resend must not become a mail cannon (mandatory). → FR: re-invite; NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Admission control — the system refuses work it cannot drain rather than queueing it forever (mandatory). → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Object store — documents kept as evidence, in the client's region (mandatory). → NFR: compliance; security & tenancy.
  • Private network — only the public edge faces the internet (mandatory). → NFR: security & tenancy.
  • Encrypted personal-data store with key custody — one envelope key per person, held where the application cannot export it (additional). → FR: separate PII store; NFR: compliance.
  • Scheduler with leader election — the sweeper and the recheck clocks run on their own clock, and two of any of them running at once double-invite people and double-spend vendor quota (additional). → FR: re-screening, re-verification, failure notice.
  • Elastic worker capacity — the recurring book outgrows live intake by an order of magnitude within three years (additional). → NFR: scale.

The numbers — every figure is per region, and the largest region dominates:

  • Writes: ~50 row-writes per flow (~12 appended events, the same number of projection updates, 7 task rows claimed and completed so twice touched, ~5 inbox rows, and one row each for the idempotency key, the invite key, the document and two audit entries) × 10k flows/day, ×2 for business-hours bunching. A single primary is untroubled below ~100 writes/s. ≈ 6 row-writes/s into Postgres, ~12/s peak — two rungs below the first number that troubles it. → NFR: scale.
  • Claim polling: 3 vendor pools × 3 replicas polling every 200 ms ≈ 45 claim queries/s against the primary. Trivial against a partial index on pending work, a growing sequential scan without one — so that index is day-one furniture rather than a scaling exit, and wake-on-notify collapses the empty polls. → NFR: scale.
  • Metadata storage: ~8 KB retained per concluded flow (~12 events at ~300 bytes each, the projection rows, and roughly half as much again in indexes) × 3.65M flows/year ≈ 29 GB/year from live intake. → NFR: scale.
  • Recheck storage: each quarterly round appends ~6 events and 4 round rows ≈ 3.6 KB. A book of 10.9M open relationships by year three, at four rounds a year, is 43.6M rounds ≈ 157 GB/year and rising with the book. This is the dominant storage term and it compounds against open relationships while revenue tracks new ones — which is why ending the obligation at relationship close is a functional requirement rather than housekeeping. → FR: relationship close; NFR: scale.
  • Documents: 2 MB × 10k/day ≈ 7.3 TB/year accruing, plateauing near 58 TB under an assumed three-year mean relationship and a five-year post-relationship retention. Traffic sets the slope; the retention clock sets the ceiling. → NFR: compliance.
  • Outbound vendor calls: live intake is 4 lists × 10k/day ≈ 0.5 legs/s. The recheck book at year three is 478k legs/day ≈ 5.5 legs/s, twelve times live intake, and the binding limit is the vendor's contracted rate rather than compute. Batching 500 persons per call takes the recurring half to under a thousand calls a day. → NFR: scale.
  • Waiting (Little's law): ~24 h mean submission wait × 10k/day ≈ 10k flows parked — inert rows the claim query never scans, resolved by the 48-hour link expiry, two automatic re-invites, and a seven-day sweep that expires the flow and tells the client. → NFR: consistency; availability & resilience.

Verdict per candidate — including the ones a bigger system would claim reflexively, so each is on the record:

  • Synchronous request/response — rejected: the person takes about a day and the vendor is down ~6 hours a week, so a connection held per in-flight flow exhausts the pool at trivial concurrency. → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Event-driven core — adopted: every wait becomes a durable row and every change an appended fact. → NFR: consistency.
  • Append-only log as the system of record — adopted: the auditor's evidence and the client's event are the same append, so there is no second write that could disagree with the first. → FR: history; NFR: consistency, compliance.
  • Folding current state from the log on every read — rejected: the dashboard and the claim query both want one row, and replaying a decade of a re-screened person's events to answer "what state is this in" costs latency for no guarantee. → FR: dashboard.
  • Same-transaction projection — adopted: the read model cannot lag the log because it commits with it, and it can be rebuilt from the log when its shape changes. → NFR: consistency.
  • A separate outbox table — rejected: with an append-only log the relay reads the log, so a second table is one more thing to keep honest for a guarantee the log already gives. → NFR: consistency.
  • Inbox keyed on the sender's own request id — adopted: a vendor's duplicate can arrive before this system has written anything, so the dedup key must be minted by the side that can see both copies. → NFR: consistency.
  • Work queue as a table in the same store — adopted: the task insert commits in the same transaction as the append that spawned it, and SKIP LOCKED is a competent competing-consumers queue at ~12 writes/s. → NFR: consistency; scale.
  • Bounded retry with a dead state on the task row — adopted: an attempt budget and a recorded last error are what turn a poison task into an operator's inbox instead of an infinite loop. → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Per-flow ordered delivery — adopted: the log's per-flow sequence is already a total order, so the relay publishes in it and the client can reject anything older than what it has applied. → FR: verdict ordering.
  • Batched vendor calls for the recurring book — adopted: it moves the binding constraint by roughly 500×, the difference between fitting the contracted rate at year three and renegotiating it. → NFR: scale.
  • Elastic worker pools driven by queue age — adopted: pools block on vendor calls, so processor utilisation says nothing and the age of the oldest pending item says everything. → NFR: scale.
  • Admission control at the edge — adopted: refusing a create with a retry hint costs the client less than accepting it into a queue already hours behind. → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Scheduler leases — adopted: the sweeper and both recheck clocks are singletons, and a second copy re-invites people and re-spends vendor quota. → FR: re-screening.
  • Separate encrypted vault with a key manager — adopted: erasure has to be provable, so it is key destruction rather than a delete somebody must be trusted to have run. → NFR: compliance.
  • Object store — adopted: evidence and residency force it, and at tens of terabytes of immutable blobs it is also the cheapest place they could live. → NFR: compliance.
  • Coordination cache — adopted: correctness, not speed — per-process breakers make N replicas absorb N times the vendor's failures, then send N probes at it as it recovers. → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Synchronous in-region standby — adopted: the standby acknowledges every commit before the client does, so promotion loses no acknowledged fact. → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Asynchronous replica — rejected: it can acknowledge a commit it never received, and the fact lost would be the append the client was already promised. → NFR: availability & resilience.
  • Message router — rejected: a task's kind already names its destination, so a router reading the field the dispatcher already reads is a component with no decision to make. → NFR: evolvability.
  • Wire tap — rejected: the log is already a complete, ordered copy of every fact, so a tap would put personal data on a second surface to report what the first one reports. → NFR: observability; compliance.
  • Distributed lock — rejected: the claim's lock lives and dies inside one transaction and nothing else here needs exclusivity that outlives one; the schedulers are the exception, and they take a lease. → NFR: consistency.
  • Read cache — rejected: the dashboard reads by primary key and the working set is one flow at a time, however large the log grows. → FR: dashboard.
  • Search index — rejected: the dashboard asks "show me this flow", never "find flows matching text". → FR: dashboard.
  • Message broker — deferred: it trades the shared transaction away to solve a throughput problem this system does not have; the trigger is measured task-table churn, roughly sustained 100k flows/day, and the log survives the move untouched. → NFR: scale.
  • Workflow engine — deferred: the log plus a task table already gives durable, inspectable orchestration; the trigger is flow variants multiplying, priced at handing the append-only history to the engine's own. → NFR: evolvability.
  • Log partitioning by time — deferred: the trigger is a region's log passing roughly 500 GB, which the recheck arithmetic reaches in year three. → NFR: scale.
  • Read replica — deferred: the trigger is dashboard and audit-export reads competing with worker claims. → NFR: scale.

When this stops being right. The recurring book wears out first, and the database is not what feels it. Every open relationship buys a quarterly round on every list forever, so outbound vendor calls grow with the accumulated book while revenue grows only with new business — twelve times live intake by year three, against a rate somebody contracted for today's volume. The signal: outbound legs per second per vendor as a fraction of the contracted ceiling, plotted against new flows per day, because the moment those two curves separate the bill has stopped tracking the business. The exits in adoption order: batch the recurring rounds, which is already adopted and buys roughly 500× on its own; stagger cadences so a jurisdiction's rounds spread across its quarter instead of landing on one day; renegotiate the contract with the growth curve in hand; then partition the log by time as a region passes ~500 GB. Behind all of them sits the cheapest exit, which is honouring relationship close — a book that retires ex-customers plateaus, and one that does not never stops growing. → NFR: scale.

Core entities & data design

One table is written and everything else is read from it: flow_event is the system of record, and the current-state row, the audit view and the client's event stream are three readings of it rather than three writes that could disagree. Two seams the schema keeps rather than a convention someone has to remember — personal data lives in a vault the operational store reaches only through an opaque person_ref, and operational bookkeeping (attempts, leases, deadlines) lives on task rows that never touch the log. Three constraints carry most of the rules: the (flow_id, seq) primary key is simultaneously the optimistic guard on every append and the order the relay delivers in, one_open_flow is the duplicate-invite rule, and the inbox's three-column uniqueness is the callback dedup. Every client-scoped table carries client_id, because a table without the column is a table no row-level-security policy can reach — the guarantee is only as wide as the column.

Tenancy & identity — operational Postgres, PII vault, key manager

Client

The tenant, and the region its data may never leave. Its row also fixes how every other table is protected: the application connects as a non-owner role so FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY actually binds it.

schema — client 
CREATE TABLE client (
  id                 uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  region             text NOT NULL,    -- recorded at onboarding; PII and documents stay in it
  webhook_url        text NOT NULL,
  webhook_secret_ref text NOT NULL     -- points into the secret store, never the secret
  -- … name, timestamps …
);

-- The same three lines go on EVERY client-scoped table, which is why every one of them
-- carries client_id. FORCE matters: without it the table owner bypasses its own policy.
ALTER TABLE flow_event ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
ALTER TABLE flow_event FORCE  ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON flow_event
  USING (client_id = current_setting('app.client_id')::uuid);

-- SET LOCAL, never SET: the setting dies with the transaction, so a pooled connection
-- cannot carry one tenant's identity into the next checkout.
-- The worker fleet claims across tenants and so cannot run under this policy. It runs as
-- a separate, separately audited principal — an exemption stated here rather than
-- discovered later (dive 7).

Person

Email and submitted personal data, sealed under a per-person envelope key. Lives in the vault, referenced only by id; erasure destroys the key rather than the row.

schema — person 
-- In the encrypted vault: a separate store, its own credentials, one per region.
CREATE TABLE person (
  id             uuid PRIMARY KEY,     -- the person_ref the operational store carries
  client_id      uuid NOT NULL,
  encrypted_data bytea NOT NULL,       -- email + submitted personal data, sealed as one blob
  key_id         text  NOT NULL        -- per-person envelope key, held by the key manager
);
-- Erasure destroys the key named by key_id and the blob becomes noise.
-- The operational store keeps email_mac, not a plain hash: an email is low-entropy and
-- enumerable, so an unkeyed digest of one is a dictionary away from re-identifying the
-- person the erasure was supposed to protect (dive 6).

MagicLinkKey

The onboardee's whole identity: a single-use invite key with a 48-hour expiry. It is spent when the document lands, not when the form posts — a link spent early strands anyone whose upload fails.

schema — magic_link_key 
CREATE TABLE magic_link_key (
  key_hash       bytea PRIMARY KEY,  -- SHA-256 of a 256-bit CSPRNG token; never the token
  client_id      uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES client(id),
  flow_id        uuid NOT NULL,
  expires_at     timestamptz NOT NULL,  -- issued_at + 48h; a predicate, not a store TTL
  spent_at       timestamptz,           -- set when the DOCUMENT lands, not at submit
  invalidated_at timestamptz            -- superseded by a resend: a different fact from spent
);

-- Redemption is a conditional write checked by rowcount, never a read-then-write:
--   UPDATE magic_link_key SET … WHERE key_hash = $h
--     AND spent_at IS NULL AND invalidated_at IS NULL AND expires_at > now();
-- Zero rows updated tells expired from forged from already-spent, and two concurrent
-- redemptions cannot both win.

PersonRelationship

What the recurring obligations hang off, and the only thing that ever ends them. Retention counts from closed_at, which is why an object-store lifecycle rule cannot be the retention policy.

schema — person_relationship 
CREATE TABLE person_relationship (
  client_id       uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES client(id),
  person_ref      uuid NOT NULL,
  opened_at       timestamptz NOT NULL,
  closed_at       timestamptz,          -- set by the client; ends every recurring obligation
  rescreen_due_at timestamptz,          -- NULLed on close, so the clock stops finding the row
  reverify_due_at timestamptz,          -- likewise
  retain_until    timestamptz,          -- computed FROM closed_at, not from the check
  PRIMARY KEY (client_id, person_ref)
);
-- Nulling the two due dates on close is the whole termination rule. Without it the book
-- only grows: the system keeps buying quarterly screens on ex-customers, and keeps
-- processing their data with no live lawful basis (Right-sizing, dive 8).
-- Retention runs on this clock. The bucket's lifecycle rule expires by OBJECT AGE and so
-- can only ever be a backstop for orphaned blobs — never the policy.

The log — operational Postgres

FlowEvent

The system of record: one append carries the state change, the auditor's evidence and the client's event. Its primary key doubles as the optimistic guard, so a stale writer collides instead of overwriting.

schema — flow_event 
CREATE TYPE flow_event_type AS ENUM (
  'flow_created', 'invite_issued', 'invite_superseded', 'submission_received',
  'document_stored', 'idv_requested', 'idv_passed', 'idv_failed',
  'screening_started', 'list_reported', 'screening_concluded',
  'reverification_started', 'relationship_closed', 'flow_expired');

CREATE TABLE flow_event (              -- append-only; nothing here is ever updated
  client_id      uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES client(id),
  flow_id        uuid NOT NULL,
  seq            int  NOT NULL,        -- per-flow order
  type           flow_event_type NOT NULL,
  payload        jsonb NOT NULL,       -- the round, the list, the vendor's verdict
  client_visible boolean NOT NULL DEFAULT false,  -- the relay publishes exactly these
  event_id       uuid NOT NULL UNIQUE, -- the client's dedup key, stable across redeliveries
  at             timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  PRIMARY KEY (flow_id, seq)
);

-- The append IS the optimistic check. A writer inserts at the seq it read, so a second
-- writer working from the same read collides on the primary key and rolls back — no
-- version column, and no lost update (dive 2).
-- seq is also the delivery order, which is what stops a stale clear overtaking a
-- sanctioned hit (dive 3).

Projections — operational Postgres

Flow

Current state, folded forward in the same transaction as the append that moves it — so it can never lag the log, and can be rebuilt from it. A re-screen never moves this row: the verdict stands until its round concludes.

schema — flow 
-- Eight states: four in flight, three verdict terminals, one abandoned terminal.
CREATE TYPE flow_state AS ENUM (
  'initiated', 'awaiting_submission', 'awaiting_id_verification', 'awaiting_screening',
  'clear', 'sanctioned', 'invalid_id',
  'expired');

CREATE TABLE flow (                    -- a PROJECTION: rebuildable, never written alone
  id           uuid PRIMARY KEY,
  client_id    uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES client(id),
  person_ref   uuid NOT NULL,          -- opaque ref into the vault
  email_mac    bytea NOT NULL,         -- HMAC under a per-region key erasure also destroys
  state        flow_state NOT NULL DEFAULT 'initiated',
  last_seq     int NOT NULL DEFAULT 0, -- how far this projection has folded
  state_due_at timestamptz             -- this state's deadline; the sweeper's predicate
);

CREATE UNIQUE INDEX one_open_flow ON flow (client_id, email_mac)
  WHERE state NOT IN ('clear', 'sanctioned', 'invalid_id', 'expired');

-- Re-SCREENING does not touch state: the flow stays 'clear' while its round runs, and
-- flips only when the round concludes. So a recheck can never make a concluded flow look
-- open and hand it back to a client who asked to start a new one.
-- Re-VERIFICATION does reopen the flow, because only the person can supply a new
-- document — and a create during that window correctly returns it, since a flow for that
-- person genuinely is open.

ScreeningRound

The fan-in tally: one row per list per round, and the collector counts within the round the fan-out minted. The round travels on the task row and in each leg's event payload, so no leg ever has to guess which round it belongs to.

schema — screening_round 
CREATE TABLE screening_round (
  client_id   uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES client(id),
  flow_id     uuid NOT NULL,
  round       int  NOT NULL,   -- minted by the screening_started event that opened it
  list        text NOT NULL,   -- one row per list the jurisdiction requires
  verdict     text,            -- NULL until this leg reports
  deadline_at timestamptz NOT NULL,   -- per-leg, so a silent vendor is a breach not a hang
  PRIMARY KEY (flow_id, round, list)
);

-- The collector's read names the round explicitly — never max(round), which races:
--   SELECT count(*) FILTER (WHERE verdict IS NULL) FROM screening_round
--   WHERE flow_id = $flow AND round = $round;
-- Zero remaining is the only thing that appends screening_concluded. Without the round,
-- the first leg of April's re-screen sees January's three verdicts still sitting there,
-- counts the round complete and concludes on last quarter's answers (dive 3).

Work & integration — operational Postgres

Task

The queue, and every operational fact the log refuses to carry: attempts, the budget that bounds them, the lease, the deadline and the last error. A task without max_attempts retries a poison message forever; one without last_error is parked where nobody can see why.

schema — task 
CREATE TABLE task (                  -- ALL operational state; none of it reaches the log
  id           bigserial PRIMARY KEY,
  client_id    uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES client(id),
  flow_id      uuid NOT NULL,
  kind         text NOT NULL,        -- send_invite | verify_id | screen_list | deliver_event
  class        text NOT NULL,        -- 'live' | 'recheck' — the reserved-capacity split
  args         jsonb NOT NULL,       -- the round, the list, the batch's member ids
  status       text NOT NULL DEFAULT 'pending',  -- pending | processing | done | dead
  attempts     int  NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  max_attempts int  NOT NULL DEFAULT 8,  -- the budget; without it, poison retries forever
  last_error   text,                  -- what an operator actually opens a dead task to read
  deadline_at  timestamptz NOT NULL,  -- this attempt's own deadline
  run_after    timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),  -- backoff WITH jitter lands here
  locked_by    text,
  lease_until  timestamptz,           -- renewed by a live worker; expiry is the sweeper's case
  dead_at      timestamptz            -- parked, alerted, re-runnable once the cause is fixed
);

CREATE INDEX task_claimable ON task (class, run_after) WHERE status = 'pending';
-- Day-one furniture, not a scaling exit: ~45 claim queries/s run against this predicate,
-- and without the index each one scans a growing prefix of completed rows.

-- so the lease, not the task, is what a stalled worker loses (dive 4).

The interface — API design

Every client-facing state-changing call returns 202: the work is recorded durably and done afterwards. The one exception is the onboardee's submission, a 201 carrying the upload URL it just created. There is no single idempotency mechanism — each boundary carries the guard held by whichever side can actually see the duplicate, so every contract below names its own. Admission control is part of the contract, not an incident: when a workload class's oldest pending item passes its ceiling, POST /flows answers 429 with a Retry-After, because accepting a flow into a queue already hours behind is a promise the system cannot keep. Nothing polls and nothing cancels: replay plus the dashboard is the entire recovery path.

How the system is built

Everything public enters through one door: clients create and erase through the Identification API, onboardees reach it through a single-use magic link, and vendors call back into it. The one structural decision is that the log is the system of record — a transition is an append to flow_event, and the current-state row, the auditor's export and the client's webhook are three readings of that one write rather than three writes that could disagree. Every wait is a durable row, never a held connection: the person takes about a day and the identity vendor is unavailable ~6 hours a week, so a connection per in-flight flow would exhaust the pool at trivial concurrency. Stateless worker pools claim the tasks each append spawns and call the vendors; a delivery relay walks each flow's log in sequence order and pushes signed webhooks; schedulers run the clocks nobody's request triggers.

Components & communication

  • API gateway + Identification API — the only surface the internet may address, and where every request enters. It terminates TLS, authenticates the tenant, enforces the per-client and per-flow limits, and answers 429 when a workload class is behind. Its one job past that is to commit facts: an append, its projection fold and its follow-up tasks, in a single transaction.
  • Postgres — the log and everything derived from it: flow_event, the flow and screening_round projections, task, inbox, delivery_cursor, idempotency_key and audit_log. Personal data is not in it. A synchronous in-region standby acknowledges every commit before the client does.
  • Worker pools — stateless replicas, one pool per vendor, claiming with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED and renewing a lease while they work. Adding capacity is adding a replica, and the pools scale on the age of their oldest pending task rather than on processor load.
  • Schedulers — the clock-driven half of the same fleet, each holding a lease so a second copy cannot run: the sweeper expires leases, dead-letters exhausted tasks and expires overdue flows, while the two recheck clocks re-enter concluded flows on each jurisdiction's cadence and stop the moment a relationship closes.
  • Delivery relay — claims a flow, not an event, and sends the lowest client-visible seq above that flow's delivered_seq. One lane per flow is what makes ordering a property of the design rather than a hope.
  • Coordination cache — breaker state, provider weights, vendor rate counters and the schedulers' leases. Coordination, not read relief: it exists so every replica sees the same open circuit and the same remaining quota.
  • PII vault + key manager — the sealed person blobs behind their own credentials, and the envelope keys the application can use but never export. Erasure is a call to destroy a key.
  • Object store — identity photos, uploaded straight from the onboardee's browser on a presigned URL. Workers pass the storage_key, never the bytes.
  • Client and onboardee — two audiences with two auth models: a tenant API key or dashboard session for the first, possession of a single-use link for the second, who has no account and should never need one.
  • IDV, sanction lists, email — the given externals, consumed behind per-vendor pools and re-entering through the inbox. The client's own webhook endpoint receives at-least-once, HMAC-signed events carrying both an eventId and a seq.

Where each requirement lands

One line per functional requirement, in the requirements block's order. Every path names only components drawn on the board.

  • Start a flow from an email — Client → API → Postgres: one transaction appends flow_created, folds the projection and inserts the invite task. → FR: start.
  • A repeated create for an open pair returns the existing flow — the one_open_flow partial unique index in Postgres, not application logic. → FR: no duplicates.

  • Self-serve re-invitation — an API endpoint invalidates prior keys and issues one fresh key, behind the gateway's per-flow limit. → FR: re-invite.
  • Verification through the given provider — Worker pools (IDV) → vendor; the callback re-enters at the API as an inbox row and its append shares that transaction. → FR: verify.
  • Screening only after verification passes — the fan-out tasks are written by the idv_passed append and by nothing else, so the ordering is structural. → FR: screen after verify.
  • A flow concludes only once every list has reported — Worker pools write leg verdicts into screening_round, and only the leg finding none still outstanding in its own round appends screening_concluded. → FR: fan-in.
  • The result on a webhook — Postgres log → Delivery relay → Client endpoint. → FR: result.
  • A repeated delivery is recognisable — the event_id on the log row travels with every redelivery. → FR: recognisable repeat.
  • Two results arrive in the order they were produced — the relay reads delivery_cursor and sends one seq at a time per flow. → FR: verdict ordering.
  • Failure reaches the client — the Schedulers append the failure event, and it leaves by the same relay path as any result. → FR: failure notice.
  • Re-screening on a per-jurisdiction cadence — the Schedulers' recheck clock reads person_relationship.rescreen_due_at and opens a new round without moving the flow's verdict. → FR: re-screening.
  • Re-verification when a document expires — the second clock re-enters at the invite, because only the person can supply a new document. → FR: re-verification.

  • Every change recorded, any history reconstructible — flow_event is the write itself, so the history is not a second artefact that could be missing. → FR: history.
  • Step-by-step dashboard view — the dashboard reads that same log through the API, under the tenant's row-level-security session. → FR: dashboard.
  • Personal data in its own store — the PII vault behind person_ref; photos in the Object store. → FR: separate PII store.

Deep dives

1 · Two workloads, one queue → NFR: scale

The recurring book is a second workload the size of the first, so it gets reserved capacity and batched calls rather than a hopeful off-peak window. At ~12 row-writes a second the queue's whole value is that a task insert shares a transaction with the append that spawned it, so scale-out is more replicas rather than more infrastructure. Worker replicas compete with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED: the claim locks a pending row and skips anything already locked, so two workers cannot take the same task and none ever blocks.

Ordering the claim by priority is the obvious reach and the wrong one. A priority queue's own counsel is to avoid strict preemption when the low-priority class also has a deadline, and re-screening has one — a jurisdiction's cadence is a legal clock. So the split is reserved capacity per class: recheck tasks carry class = 'recheck' and are claimed by a separately sized pool, which is the per-vendor bulkhead applied a second time, per workload. Live flows keep a floor the batch cannot borrow; the batch keeps a floor a busy onboarding day cannot starve. The vendor quota is split the same way, because two pools drawing on one ceiling are not two pools: on a delta day the batch would spend the whole contracted rate and live legs would be pushed out on run_after — starvation at the quota rather than at the pool, with aggregate alarms green because the batch is draining at full speed.

Batching is what keeps the bill survivable, and it is priced rather than assumed. Per-person legs put 5.5 outbound calls a second against a contracted rate negotiated for today's volume; 500 persons per call takes the recurring half to under a thousand calls a day. The cost is partial failure — one bad record in a batch of 500 — so the batch response is applied one member per transaction, and a member that fails dead-letters its own leg while the other 499 commit. That is the only reason batching and per-task dead-lettering coexist here: the batch is a transport optimisation, never a unit of failure.

Capacity moves on queue age, not processor load. These pools sit blocked on vendor calls, so utilisation stays near zero while the backlog grows — scaling on it would never fire. Each pool scales out when its oldest pending task passes 60 seconds and back in after ten minutes below five, with a floor of two replicas for redundancy and a ceiling set by the vendor's contracted concurrency rather than by our budget. Scale-in is the dangerous direction: a stopping replica finishes or abandons its claim, and an abandoned claim is simply the expired-lease case the sweeper already handles, so a rolling deploy needs no drain choreography the failure path had not already rehearsed.

2 · One append, or nothing → NFR: consistency

Every guarantee on this page reduces to one rule: the state change, the evidence and the client's event are the same row, so there is no second write that could be missing. V1 of this design wrote the transition and then an outbox row in the same transaction, which is correct and costs one more table to keep honest. With a log there is nothing to keep honest — the relay reads the log. The residual weakness is real and worth naming: the log is now load-bearing for four consumers at once, so a schema mistake in payload is a mistake in the audit record, the projection and the webhook simultaneously.

The append is its own optimistic guard. A writer reads the flow's last_seq, does its work, and inserts at seq + 1. A second writer working from the same read collides on the (flow_id, seq) primary key and rolls back — no version column, no lost update, and a zombie worker whose lease the sweeper already expired finds its append rejected rather than double-applied. That is optimistic concurrency where conflicts are rare, which they are: two writers on one flow means a retry raced a recovery. The claim query is the opposite case and takes the opposite tool — SKIP LOCKED is pessimistic locking, because there contention is the normal condition rather than the exception. Neither is a distributed lock: both live and die inside one transaction, and nothing here needs exclusivity that outlives one. The schedulers are the single exception, and they take a lease.

Duplicates are guarded at the boundary that can see them. A vendor's retry can arrive before this system has written anything, so a key we mint at publish time finds nothing to collide with and both copies apply. The inbox keys on the sender's own request id, and its insert shares the transaction with the append it causes: whichever copy commits first wins, the loser hits the unique constraint and takes its whole transaction down, append included. Four boundaries, four keys, each minted by whichever side can actually see the duplicate — the client's stored Idempotency-Key, the vendor's requestId, the task row's id, and the event_id the client dedups on.

The fan-in blocks the conclusion, and the round is what makes it honest. Screening fans one leg per list, and only the leg that finds none still outstanding within its own round appends screening_concluded. Drop the round and April's first returning leg sees January's three verdicts still sitting in their columns, counts the round complete, and concludes on last quarter's answers — the one failure this design must never have, and the reason round travels on the task row and in every leg's payload rather than being re-derived with max().

Order is a separate guarantee from dedup, and conflating them is how a client ends up believing the wrong verdict. A re-screen flips a flow from clear to sanctioned; the sanctioned delivery gets a 502 and enters backoff; a later correction delivers first. Both carry distinct eventIds, so the client's dedup absorbs neither and arrival order decides. The fix is that the relay claims a flow rather than an event and sends one seq at a time: a flow in backoff blocks its own lane and nobody else's. The client also gets seq in the payload, so it can discard anything older than what it has applied even if the network reorders below us.

CAP, applied rather than recited — choose C. During a partition the single-writer Postgres refuses creates rather than risk two open flows for one client-and-email pair, or an append the relay never sees. Stalled work parks in the queue and drains on recovery, exactly as after a vendor outage. PACELC reads PC/EC: pay latency in every branch, never inconsistency. The one eventually-consistent surface is the client's view — an at-least-once webhook that may lag by minutes — and it is accepted up front rather than discovered.

3 · Service levels, poison messages and the failure budget → NFR: availability & resilience

You can only promise what you own, and this system does not own the thing that takes longest. The identity vendor is unavailable about six hours a week, which is 3.6% of the clock, and the person takes roughly a day to submit. Any promise about end-to-end completion is therefore a promise about someone else's uptime and a stranger's diary, so the contract is written about what the system controls: that nothing is silently dropped, that escalation happens on a stated clock, and that the client hears about every terminal outcome. That distinction is the whole reason the three terms below are kept apart rather than used interchangeably.

The indicator is what you measure. Four of them here, each a quantity with an owner: a flow's age in its current state, a task's age since it became claimable, delivery attempts outstanding per flow, and per-vendor error rate. Each is a column or a count, not a derived score — a service level built on something nobody can query is an aspiration.

The objective is what you engineer to, and it is internal. Assumed, not given: 99% of flows leave awaiting_id_verification within 4 hours of the vendor accepting them; 99% of screening rounds close within 12 hours of fan-out; 99.9% of client-visible events are delivered within 5 minutes of their append. These are the numbers the alarms are set against and the numbers a deploy is allowed to spend against.

The agreement is what you promise the client, with a consequence attached, and it must be looser than the objective or there is no room to operate. Assumed, not given: an operator is paged within 15 minutes of a flow passing its state deadline, and the client receives a failure event within 5 minutes of a flow being declared stuck. The gap between the objective and the agreement is the error budget — spend it on deploys, vendor outages and the occasional bad batch, and when it is exhausted the correct response is to stop shipping rather than to restate the number. Note what is deliberately absent: there is no agreement on how fast a verdict arrives, because the two slowest participants are a third party and a human being.

The deadlines those levels are enforced against are columns, not conventions. Each state carries a state_due_at and each task a deadline_at: 48 hours on an invitation link with two automatic re-invites and a seven-day expiry on the flow, 24 hours on a verification callback, 4 hours on a screening leg with the round breaching at 12. The sweeper runs every 60 seconds against those predicates, which is what makes the 15-minute page achievable rather than aspirational.

A poison task is bounded work, not an infinite loop. attempts counts, max_attempts stops, last_error is what an operator opens the row to read, and dead_at parks it where it is alerted and re-runnable once the cause is fixed. Without the budget a malformed vendor response burns a worker slot forever; without the recorded error the dead task is a number with no diagnosis. Backoff carries jitter, because a cohort that failed in the same instant otherwise computes the same delay and returns in the same instant — a six-hour vendor outage would end in a synchronised stampede at the moment of recovery.

Around each vendor sit four controls that answer four different questions. A timeout and deadline bound one call, because no vendor publishes a latency guarantee and the sweeper needs a line to enforce. A rate limiter stops calls that would succeed but exceed the contract. A circuit breaker stops calls that would fail, with its state in the shared cache: per-process breakers make N replicas absorb N times the failures before the last one opens, then send N probes at a recovering vendor. And a bulkhead keeps a stalled vendor from consuming the pool another vendor needs. When the cache is unreachable each worker fails closed to a local breaker and a local token bucket at its fair share; when the cache comes back empty — a restart or a failover — breakers seed open rather than closed, because reading an empty cache as "all healthy" is how the whole fleet stampedes a vendor that is still down.

Backpressure closes the loop the queue leaves open. A queue absorbs a burst; it does nothing about sustained overload except postpone the failure and grow. So the accept path is coupled to the drain rate: when the live class's oldest pending task passes its ceiling, POST /flows answers 429 with a Retry-After. Refusing costs the client a retry; accepting costs them a flow that sits invisible for hours behind work they cannot see. Below the database, the standby acknowledges every commit before the client does, so a failover loses no acknowledged fact — and an asynchronous replica is rejected precisely because the fact it could drop is the append the client was already promised.

4 · What you watch, and why there is no tap → NFR: observability

The log is already the trace, so observability here is one correlation id, a small alarm set, and SQL — not a second copy of the traffic. The flow id threads every append, task, vendor call and delivery attempt, so "why is this flow stuck" is one query rather than an archaeology exercise across services. At ~75 flows a day and one database, distributed tracing would instrument a call graph three hops deep; the correlation id and the log cover it, and the honest note is that this stops being true the moment the deferred broker arrives and work leaves the transaction.

A wire tap is the reach this design rejects, and the reason is not cost. Copying every message to a side channel is the standard answer for visibility without touching producer or consumer — but the log already is a complete, ordered, immutable copy of every fact, queryable in place. A tap would add nothing except a second surface carrying personal data, and a monitoring surface is exactly where access controls are weakest. The pattern's own counsel is to avoid it when the payload is sensitive and the tap channel is not secured to the same standard. Where a tap would genuinely earn its place is shadow-testing a new sanction-list vendor against live traffic, and that is a named future need rather than a reason to build one now.

Six alarms, each with its runbook shipped alongside the definition rather than in a wiki that drifts: oldest pending task age per class, because an aggregate stays green while the live class starves; flows past their state deadline; dead tasks above zero, which is always a page; delivery lanes in backoff, grouped by tenant so one dead endpoint reads as one problem; per-vendor error rate feeding both the alert and the breaker weights; and outbound legs per second against the contracted ceiling, which is the one alarm that fires months before anything breaks. Logs carry ids only — the vault is the single place raw personal data exists, and a log line is not permitted to become a second copy of it.

5 · Retention, erasure, and the three clocks → NFR: compliance

Three different clocks run over the same person's data, they pull against each other, and the design's job is to say which one wins rather than to pretend they agree. The invitation expiry is a security control measured in hours. The retention obligation is a legal duty measured in years and counted from the end of the relationship. The erasure request is a data-subject right that arrives whenever it arrives. Collapsing them into one "cleanup job" is how a system either destroys evidence it was required to keep or keeps data it was required to destroy.

Retention counts from closed_at, which is why an object-store lifecycle rule cannot be the policy. A lifecycle rule expires by object age: it would delete a photo five years after it was taken, while the obligation runs five years after the merchant stopped trading with that person — dates that can differ by a decade. So person_relationship.retain_until is computed at close on the jurisdiction's schedule, a retention sweep enforces it on the same due-dated-predicate machinery as the recheck clocks, and the bucket's lifecycle rule stays as a backstop for genuinely orphaned blobs. Naming one mechanism the policy and the other a backstop is the part that matters; a page that says both is a page that has decided neither.

Erasure is key destruction, because a delete is a claim and a destroyed key is a proof. Personal data is sealed under a per-person envelope key held by the key manager, so destroying that key makes every copy unreadable at once — including the copies in backups, which no DELETE can reach without restoring and rewriting them. The identity photo is destroyed the same way rather than deleted, so object versions and bucket backups go with it. And flow.email_mac is an HMAC under a per-region key that erasure also destroys: an email address is low-entropy and enumerable, so a plain digest of one would let anyone with database access recover which named individuals had been screened, by which client, to what verdict — an erasure that leaves the index intact has not erased anything.

Retention outranks erasure while it is live, and the request is queued rather than refused. An onboardee's erasure arriving while their merchant is still under a statutory obligation cannot simply win, so DELETE /persons/{ref} answers 409 with the date the obligation lapses and schedules the destruction for that date. The client can then give their data subject an answer instead of an apology. Two things deliberately survive the erasure: flow_event and audit_log, both of which reference only the opaque person_ref. Destroying the papertrail alongside the person would erase the single piece of evidence that the erasure ever happened, which is the outcome the requirement exists to prevent.

6 · Tenancy the database enforces → NFR: security & tenancy

An application filter is one forgotten WHERE client_id = away from a reportable incident, so the database refuses the unscoped read no matter which code path issued it. That guarantee is only as wide as the column it binds to, which is why client_id is on every client-scoped table rather than on the three that obviously needed it. A history table without the column is a history table the policy cannot cover, and transition histories leak verdicts and timestamps just as effectively as names do.

Three details decide whether the policy actually holds, and each is a way it silently does not. ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY alone is bypassed by the table owner, so the application connects as a non-owner role and the tables carry FORCE ROW LEVEL SECURITY. The tenant is set with SET LOCAL rather than SET, so the setting dies with the transaction instead of riding a pooled connection into the next tenant's checkout. And the worker fleet claims tasks across all tenants, so it cannot run under the policy at all — that is stated here as a separately audited principal with its own credentials and no dashboard path, rather than left as an exemption someone discovers during an incident.

The onboardee has no account and should never need one. Their identity is possession of a single-use link: 256 bits from a cryptographic generator, stored only as its hash, expiring in 48 hours, revoked the moment a resend supersedes it, and spent when the document lands rather than when the form posts. Redemption is a conditional write checked by rowcount, so two concurrent redemptions cannot both win and the failure tells expired from forged from already-spent. Its scope is exactly one flow's submission and nothing client-scoped.

Residency is one full stack per region, not a column. Gateway, API, workers, schedulers, Postgres and standby, vault, key manager, object store and cache are stamped from the same infrastructure code, and only jurisdiction configuration varies between them. Regions share no data, which is what makes the guarantee checkable — a query cannot cross a boundary that has no connection across it. The bill is stated in the trade-offs: N regions multiply cost, operational surface and configuration drift, and a regional outage is downtime for that region's clients by choice.

7 · What changes without a rewrite → NFR: evolvability

A deployment meeting only the ten mandatory requirements is a complete, correct product, and every later obligation attaches to the log the mandatory flow already writes. Re-screening, re-verification, relationship close and the access papertrail all read or append to flow_event; none of them needed a new write path, which is what makes them additions rather than a second system. A new jurisdiction is a cadence and a list set in configuration; a new sanction list is a row in that set and a new task kind its pool already understands.

Message routing is the component this design keeps not needing. A task's kind already names its destination and the claim predicate already reads it, so a router inspecting that field to decide where the work goes would be a box with no decision to make — and the routing pattern's own guidance is to skip it when the destination is knowable from a field you already have. If channels ever multiply past what one column can express, the honest answer is a broker with typed channels rather than a router bolted onto a table, and that is the deferred exit below rather than a component to add now.

The schema evolves reader-first: a new event type or task kind ships to the workers that understand it before the writer that emits it, and the projection is rebuilt from the log rather than migrated in place — which is the one operational superpower the log buys and the row-based design does not. The cost is that payload is an untyped contract four consumers depend on, so it needs the discipline a schema registry would otherwise enforce.

Every exit is additive, and each has a trigger rather than a hunch. The partial index and the day-one wake-on-notify are already in. Then: stagger recheck cadences across the quarter when the delta-day spike shows up in per-class age; partition the log by time as a region passes ~500 GB; a read replica when dashboard and audit-export reads compete with worker claims; a broker when task-table churn is measured, roughly sustained 100k flows a day, and the log survives that move untouched because the relay reads it either way; a workflow engine only if flow variants multiply, priced at handing the append-only history to the engine's own. Nothing on that path is a rewrite, which was the point of putting the log at the centre rather than a mutable row.

Limitations & trade-offs

The biggest flaw, named first: the log is load-bearing for four consumers at once — the projection, the dashboard, the auditor's export and the client's webhook all read one table, so a mistake in an event's payload is a mistake in all four at the same moment. Every risk below follows from putting one write at the centre on purpose.

Strengths

  • Nothing can diverge, because nothing is written twice. The state change, the evidence and the client's event are one append, so there is no window in which one exists without the others.
  • Order is a property, not a hope. The per-flow sequence is a total order and the relay walks one lane at a time, so a stale verdict physically cannot overtake a fresh one.
  • The concurrency guard costs nothing extra. The primary key that orders the log is the same key that rejects a stale writer — no version column, no lost update.
  • Waits are rows. A person taking a day or a vendor down six hours costs storage, never connections, threads or timeouts.
  • The recurring obligation has a stop. Closing a relationship nulls both clocks, which bounds the one quantity that otherwise grows forever and takes the vendor bill with it.
  • A projection whose shape changes is replayed from the log rather than migrated in place.
  • Every exit is additive. The broker, the replica, the partition and the engine all slot in without touching the log.

Risks

  • The log is four contracts in one untyped column. payload needs the discipline a schema registry would otherwise enforce, and nothing in the build checks it (see dive 7).
  • Postgres is the single writer and the single point of failure. Consistency was chosen over availability: an outage stalls every write until the standby is promoted (see dive 3).
  • The recurring book runs out before the database does. Vendor calls compound against open relationships while revenue tracks new ones, and a contract signed for today's volume is the thing that breaks (see dive 1).
  • Personal data concentrates blast radius in one vault and one key manager per region (see dive 5).
  • Each region is an island by requirement. A regional outage is downtime for that region's clients, and N regions multiply cost, operational surface and configuration drift.
  • The log only grows. Storage is a standing cost with a partition exit rather than a delete, because what makes it big is the evidence the design exists to keep.
  • Batching buys the vendor bill at the price of a coarser failure unit: a batch that fails in transport retries 500 people's legs rather than one.
