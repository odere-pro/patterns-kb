Theme

Event Storming

A workshop that puts everyone who knows something about the domain in one room and asks them to write down what happens, in the past tense, on a timeline — and then reads the structure of the system off the wall they built.

Theme

The question

Every pattern in the domain-driven band assumes you already know where the boundaries are. Event storming is how you find out. Alberto Brandolini's format is deliberately low-tech: a long wall, sticky notes in a few agreed colours, and everyone who knows part of the domain in the room at once — the people who use the system as much as the people who build it.

It starts with one instruction. Write down things that have happened, in the past tense, one per note: order placed, payment captured, shipment dispatched. No structure, no discussion, no ordering — just volume. Then arrange them on a timeline, and the arguments start, which is the point: two people disagreeing about the order of two events have found a misunderstanding that would otherwise have become a defect.

Structure is added in passes. Commands go before the events they cause, actors beside the commands, external systems where the flow leaves the room, and policies wherever "whenever this happens, that must follow" is spoken aloud. Aggregates appear late, as the clusters of events that share a consistency rule. Hot spots — the notes everyone argues about — get a distinct colour and are left unresolved on purpose, because an unresolved argument on the wall is worth more than a premature answer in a document.

What makes it worth a day of expensive people's time is that it produces a shared vocabulary rather than a document. The words on the wall are the words the domain experts used, so they become the words in the code — which is the whole ambition of a ubiquitous language, achieved as a by-product rather than as an initiative. The three commonly-named scales are worth distinguishing: big picture for a whole business, run once; process modelling for one flow in detail; and design-level for one aggregate, run at the whiteboard by the people implementing it, often in an hour.

Explained at three levels

Basic

Before anyone can decide how to build a system, the people involved have to agree on what actually happens in the business — and they usually do not, without knowing it. Put everyone who knows part of it in one room with a long wall and sticky notes, and ask them to write down things that have happened, in the past tense, one per note. Then put the notes in time order. The arguments that start are the point: two people ordering the same two events differently have found a misunderstanding that would otherwise have shipped as a bug.

Advanced

Structure arrives in passes rather than up front. Commands go before the events they cause, actors beside the commands, external systems where the flow leaves the room, and policies wherever someone says whenever this happens, that must follow. Aggregates are noticed late, as clusters of events sharing one consistency rule, and context boundaries show up as points on the timeline where a word changes meaning. Disagreements that cannot be settled become hot-spot notes and stay unresolved on purpose, because a marked gap is more useful than an invented answer.

Expert

Pick the scale by what is unclear rather than by how much time you have: big picture when two teams use the same word differently, process modelling when one flow crosses systems and nobody owns it end to end, design level when a single team cannot agree what one transaction covers. Two costs with counter-moves. The output is understanding held by the people in the room, and a photograph of the wall transfers almost none of it — pay that by having the people who will build it in the room, not a proxy who reports back. And a session that models cleanly in events makes event sourcing look inevitable, which is the trap: the technique is a modelling tool, and how you store the result is a separate decision with a much larger bill. Discover in events; then decide about persistence on its own merits.

The trade-space

The cost is the room. A big-picture session needs a day from people whose time is expensive and who do not normally sit together, and the output is not a specification — it is a wall of notes plus the understanding the people in the room now share. That understanding does not transfer to anyone who was absent, which is the honest limit of the technique and the reason a photograph of the wall is nearly worthless to a newcomer.

The other trade is when to stop. Every session reaches a point where the remaining disagreements are genuinely hard, and there are two ways to spend the next hour: resolve them, or write them on a hot-spot note and move on. Resolving them in the room is tempting and usually wrong — the questions that survive an event storm are the ones that need data, a customer, or a decision from someone not present, and forcing an answer produces a confident wrong model rather than a known gap.

Scale is the third axis, and picking wrong wastes the most. A big-picture session over a domain nobody disputes produces a wall everybody nods at and no information. A design-level session before the boundaries are agreed produces a detailed model of the wrong aggregate. The signal for the coarse version is that two teams use the same word differently; the signal for the fine version is that one team cannot decide what a single transaction covers.

What the wall turns into

Domain Event

The orange note is the pattern. A past-tense statement of something the business considers to have happened is exactly what the workshop asks people to write, which is why the technique produces domain events rather than requirements — and why the names survive into the code unchanged.

Aggregate

Aggregates are not drawn, they are noticed: a group of events that must agree with each other, guarded by the same rule, accepting the same commands. Finding them late in the session rather than early is deliberate, because a boundary proposed before the events are on the wall is a guess.

Bounded Context

The clearest signal on a wall is a word that changes meaning partway along it — an "order" in fulfilment is not the "order" in billing. That point is a context boundary, and it is visible in a way no amount of up-front modelling makes visible.

Anti-Corruption Layer

Once the boundary is on the wall, the notes crossing it are the integration, and the ones arriving from a system you do not control are where a translation layer earns its keep. The workshop names the traffic; this pattern is what you build for it.

Event Sourcing

A session that produces a good event model makes this look inevitable, and that is a trap worth naming: discovering the domain in events does not oblige you to store it in events. The technique is a modelling tool, and the storage decision is separate and much more expensive.

CQRS

The questions people ask during a session — "how would you know that?", "where do you look that up?" — are read models. They rarely line up with the aggregates beside them, which is the observation that makes separating the two sides worth its cost.
