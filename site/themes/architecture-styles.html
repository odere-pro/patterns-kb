Architecture Styles

An architecture style is a family of architectures sharing one set of constraints on which elements may appear and how they may relate. Honour the constraints and specific properties emerge; conform in name only and none of them do. This theme walks the six styles, the axes on which each one charges you, and how to choose for one subdomain at a time.

Theme Constraints · Consequences

The question

Two teams build the same application and end up with systems that share no shape: one is a stack of layers shipped as a single deployable, the other is forty services trading messages over a broker. Neither team chose a diagram. Each chose a set of constraints — which kinds of element may exist, and how they are allowed to relate — and the diagram followed from them. That constraint set is the architecture style, and a style needs no particular technology, though some technologies suit some styles.

Constraints buy properties. Say that a service owns one business capability, is independent of every other, and keeps its data private, and you can deploy one service without redeploying the rest, contain a failure to the service that had it, release weekly, and adopt a new runtime in one place. Relax any of the three and the matching property leaves with it: services that share a database deploy together, fail together, and have bought you only the network hops.

So the work is not picking a diagram. It is deciding which constraints this domain can honour and what you will pay for them — and then being practical, because relaxing a constraint deliberately beats chasing architectural purity, as long as you can name the property you just gave up.

Explained at three levels

Basic

A team copies a system shape they liked, then quietly breaks its rules — one component reads straight out of another's database because that was faster this week. A year later nothing can be released on its own, and nobody can point to the day that became true. The shape was never the picture; it was the rules, and the benefits last exactly as long as you keep them.

Advanced

So a style is a constraint set rather than a topology: it narrows which elements may exist and how they may relate, and the properties you wanted are consequences of the narrowing. Six recur — horizontal layers inside one deployable; a front end, a queue and a worker; functionally decomposed services behind APIs; producers and consumers joined by a broker; a partitioned dataset processed in batch and in stream; and one problem split across thousands of cores. Each manages dependencies a different way, which is why each suits a different kind of domain.

Expert

Choose by the domain and by prioritised architecture characteristics rather than by ambition, and match the architecture's complexity to the domain's: too little leaves dependencies unmanaged, too much charges for coordination the business never asked for. Every style past the simplest bills on four axes, and each bill has a counter-move — asynchronous messaging buys decoupling and charges eventual consistency, paid with idempotent consumers and explicit compensation; decomposition buys independent release and charges latency and network congestion, paid by merging the two services whose calls turned chatty; distribution buys fault isolation and charges manageability, paid with correlated logging and tracing agreed before the first service ships. Re-decide on a schedule, and expect the re-decision to be expensive — which is the argument for spending longer on the first one.

The trade-space

Constraints create challenges as well as benefits. Weigh a style for one subdomain and one bounded context rather than for the organization at large, and weigh it on the axes below — where every style past the simplest charges you, whether or not you planned for the charge.

  • Complexity. Match the architecture's complexity to the domain's. Too simple and dependencies go unmanaged until the structure decays into a Big Ball of Mud; too elaborate for the domain and you pay coordination costs nobody in the business asked for.
  • Asynchronous messaging and eventual consistency. A broker between two components decouples their lifecycles and makes a failed message retryable, so neither has to be running when the other is. In exchange every consumer must tolerate duplicate delivery, and every reader must tolerate a view that is behind.
  • Interservice communication. Each split turns function calls into network calls. Chains of them surface as latency a user feels and as congestion the platform bills for, and two components that talk constantly are telling you the boundary between them is wrong.
  • Manageability. Deploying, monitoring and keeping N services healthy is not N times the work of keeping one healthy — it is a different job, needing correlated logs, distributed tracing and automated rollout. Adopt a distributed style without them and your first incident is unreadable.

Prioritise before you pick. Name the business drivers, translate them into architecture characteristics — a hard launch date pushes maintainability, testability and reliability up the list, while a tight budget pushes feasibility and simplicity up instead — and rank them with the stakeholders who own the workload rather than with the architects alone.

Then treat the choice as revisable. Measure the characteristics you ranked, check that the constraints are still being honoured, and revise — and because changing architectural direction later is expensive, spend more on the first decision than feels comfortable. Styles combine rather than compete: event-driven is commonly the primary style, with services, pipes and filters or event sourcing layered into it.

The six styles

Layered

Divide the application into horizontal layers — presentation, business logic, data access — each with one responsibility, each calling only into the layer below. Every mainstream framework ships this shape already, so a new hire is productive on day one. The bill is agility: a horizontal split means one feature cuts across every layer, so change ripples the full height of the stack.

Web-Queue-Worker

A web front end takes requests, a queue holds the work, and a back-end worker does whatever is resource-intensive, long-running or batch. The two halves scale independently and neither blocks the other, which is the whole benefit. Left unwatched, both halves accrete features until each is a monolith of its own.

Microservices

Decompose functionally instead of horizontally: small autonomous services, one business capability each inside one bounded context, each owning its data, talking through APIs, deployed on their own schedules. Teams then ship without coordinating releases. In return you take on service discovery, cross-service data consistency and distributed management as real, staffed work.

Event-Driven

Producers emit streams of events and consumers react in near real time, joined by a channel rather than by an address. Adding a consumer touches no producer, and a consumer that is down delays nobody. What you inherit is delivery and ordering as explicit design problems, plus eventual consistency in every view built from the stream.

Big Data

Divide a dataset too large for one traditional database into chunks and process them where they sit: batch paths over history, stream paths for immediate insight, and analytical stores shaped for the questions reporting asks. The style exists because the data outgrew the query engine, not because the domain is complicated.

Big Compute

Split one computationally intensive problem into discrete tasks, allocate thousands of cores to them, then release the cores when the run finishes. Simulation and rendering fit it, and the design questions are task granularity and how results are gathered — not how services talk.

How to decide

Start from the nature of the problem, and read the table by its middle column: a style is mostly a claim about how dependencies are managed, and the domain that suits it is the one whose dependencies already look like that.
